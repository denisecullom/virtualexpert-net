How do virtual assistants use AI to help clients hire?

When a small business hires, the owner ends up buried in admin: writing the post, reading every application, scheduling calls, sending updates. A VA can take most of that off their plate, and AI makes it faster. But hiring decisions affect real people, so the line between what AI does and what the client decides has to be clear.

By Denise Cullom · November 19, 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer

Start with a role brief from the client: what the person will do, the must-have skills, pay range, hours and how to apply. Use AI to draft a clear, plain job post from it. Track applicants in one sheet. AI can help you summarize each application against the client's criteria, but the client decides who moves forward: don't let AI rank, score or reject people. Schedule interviews, prepare a question sheet for each one, and send every candidate a reply, including the ones who weren't chosen. Keep applicant data in the client's accounts.

Hiring admin is a good fit for a VA. It's time-consuming, it follows a clear process, and the client is usually glad to hand it over. AI helps with the writing and the organizing. It shouldn't help with the deciding. Automated hiring tools can repeat bias in the data they learned from, and some places now regulate their use in hiring decisions. The safe and fair approach is simple: AI handles the paperwork, people make the choices.

What goes in a role brief?

Get these from the client before you write anything:

  1. The job: the five to seven things the person will actually do each week.
  2. Must-haves: the skills or experience they can't do without. Keep this list short.
  3. Nice-to-haves: useful but not required.
  4. Pay range, hours and location, or remote.
  5. How to apply, and the closing date.
  6. The steps: application, short call, interview, task or trial, offer.
  7. Who decides at each step.

The must-have list matters most. It's what every application gets compared against, so it needs to be the client's words, agreed before anyone applies.

How do you write the job post with AI?

Give AI the role brief and ask for a post that leads with what the job is, then what the person will do, the must-haves, pay and hours, and how to apply. Plain words, short sentences, no "rockstar" or "ninja." Ask it to flag any wording that could put off qualified people for reasons unrelated to the job, such as requirements that aren't really needed.

Then edit. Check the pay, hours and closing date against the brief, and get the client's approval before it's posted.

How do you organize applicants?

Use one sheet, ideally in the client's own account or hiring tool: name, date applied, where they found the post, and a column for each must-have. As applications come in, note whether each one shows each must-have, with a short quote from the application as evidence.

AI can speed this up by summarizing an application against the criteria. Before you do that, check the client is fine with it and that the AI tool doesn't use your data for training. Remove contact details first, since the summary doesn't need them. The rules in keeping client data safe apply here, and applicant data deserves extra care.

Where must AI not decide?

  • No ranking or scoring of candidates by AI.
  • No automatic rejections. Every "no" is the client's decision.
  • No guessing about age, gender, health, family, background or anything not related to the job.
  • No judging video or voice recordings by AI.

Your job is to show the client, for each applicant, what the application says against each must-have. The client reads it and decides. Keep a note of who made each decision and when.

How do you handle interviews?

Send shortlisted candidates a scheduling link or two or three time options, with the length, format and who they'll meet. Use the client's calendar rules (see AI for calendar management).

For each interview, prepare a one-page sheet: the role summary, the candidate's application notes, and the same core questions for every candidate, based on the must-haves. Asking everyone the same questions makes the comparison fairer and easier. If the client records interviews, they need the candidate's consent, and AI notes from the recording go to the client as notes, not as a verdict.

How do you keep candidates updated?

Every applicant should hear back. Write templates for each step: application received, invited to interview, interview confirmed, not moving forward, and offer. AI drafts them once in the client's voice, and you personalize the name and role each time. A kind, prompt "no" protects the client's reputation, and in a small town or industry, it matters.

Once someone is hired, the same process feeds onboarding. The checklist and first-week plan can be written as an SOP (see writing SOPs with AI).

Which prompts should you use?

The first writes the job post:

1

Write the job post

Prompt

Write a job post from this role brief: [paste brief]. Order: one-line summary of the job, what you'll do each week, must-haves, nice-to-haves, pay range and hours, how to apply and closing date. Plain English, short sentences, no buzzwords. Under 350 words. Then list any wording or requirement that might put off qualified applicants for reasons that aren't needed for the job.

The second summarizes an application against the criteria, without ranking:

2

Summarize an application against the must-haves

Prompt

Here are the must-haves for a role: [paste list]. Here is an application with contact details removed: [paste]. For each must-have, say whether the application shows it, quote the line that shows it, or write "not mentioned." Don't score, rank or recommend. Don't comment on anything not related to the must-haves, including age, gender, background, gaps or personal details.

The third writes the candidate emails:

3

Write the candidate email templates

Prompt

Write five short email templates for my client's hiring process: application received, invited to interview (with placeholders for times and format), interview confirmed, not moving forward, and offer (placeholders only, the client fills in terms). Tone: [client's tone]. Warm, clear and under 100 words each. The "not moving forward" email should be kind and final, with no reasons given.

Frequently asked questions

Can a virtual assistant use AI to help a client hire?

Yes, for the admin: writing the job post from the client's role brief, organizing applicants in one sheet, summarizing applications against the client's must-haves, scheduling interviews, preparing question sheets and sending candidate emails. The client makes every hiring decision.

Should AI screen or rank job applicants?

No. Don't let AI rank, score or reject candidates. Automated hiring tools can repeat bias, and some places regulate them. Use AI only to summarize what each application shows against the agreed criteria, and let the client decide.

How do you keep applicant data safe?

Keep it in the client's own accounts or hiring tool, remove contact details before pasting anything into an AI tool, use a tool that doesn't train on your data, and check that the client is comfortable with AI being used at all.

What goes in a role brief?

What the person will do each week, a short list of must-haves, nice-to-haves, pay range, hours, location, how to apply, the closing date, the hiring steps and who decides at each step.

Should every job applicant get a reply?

Yes. A short, kind reply at each step, including a clear "not moving forward" message, protects the client's reputation. Templates written once make it quick.
