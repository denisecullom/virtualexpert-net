What AI services can virtual assistants sell?

Clients don't buy “AI.” They buy a cleaner inbox, follow-ups that actually go out and a Monday report they can read in five minutes. Here are ten AI services a virtual assistant can sell to small businesses, what each one fixes and what to charge to start.

By Denise Cullom · November 7, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

Ten AI services virtual assistants can sell to small businesses right now: 1. inbox triage and draft replies, 2. meeting notes and follow-up, 3. content repurposing, 4. customer support first drafts, 5. bookkeeping prep, 6. a weekly business report, 7. lead research and CRM cleanup, 8. standard operating procedures, 9. newsletter drafting, and 10. new client onboarding packs. Each one is a recurring job AI does most of while the VA sets it up, checks the output and keeps it running. Typical starting prices for a US small-business client are $250 to $900 to set up and $100 to $600 a month to run. Start with the service that fixes something a current client has complained about in the last month.

If you've followed this series from how to become an AI virtual assistant, you know the move: stop selling hours, start selling a result that AI helps you deliver every week. This is the menu of results small businesses will actually pay for.

Each one below runs on the short tool stack from the best AI tools for VAs, in either tool from ChatGPT vs. Claude. Prices are starting points for a VA working with US small businesses; adjust them to your market.

What makes a good AI service to sell?

The best ones share four traits. The job happens often (daily or weekly), it follows rules you can write down, the client hates doing it, and the result is easy to see. If a task scores well on all four, it's a service. If it's a one-off judgment call, it isn't.

1. Inbox triage and draft replies

Every morning, AI sorts the inbox into urgent, needs a reply, FYI and ignore, and drafts replies to the routine messages for approval. Who buys it: founders, consultants, agency owners, anyone who says “email is eating my day.” Starting price: about $300 to $600 setup, $150 to $300 a month.

2. Meeting notes and follow-up

After every call, the transcript becomes a summary, a list of action items with owners and deadlines, and a draft follow-up email. Who buys it: anyone who sells on calls or runs a team. Starting price: about $250 to $500 setup, $100 to $250 a month. It's the easiest first service to demonstrate, because the before and after is obvious from one meeting.

3. Content repurposing

One podcast, video, webinar or long post becomes a week of social posts, an email and a short article, in the client's voice. Who buys it: coaches, consultants and experts who create but never get round to distributing. Starting price: about $400 to $800 setup, $300 to $600 a month. Most of the setup time is teaching the AI the client's voice.

4. Customer support first drafts

AI drafts answers to common customer questions from the client's own FAQ, policies and past replies; a person approves each one. Who buys it: online stores, course creators, service businesses with a busy help inbox. Starting price: about $500 to $900 setup, $200 to $400 a month.

5. Bookkeeping prep

AI reads receipts and statements, suggests categories and flags anything unusual, so the books are ready for the client or their bookkeeper to approve. Who buys it: owners who dread month-end and pay their accountant to sort a shoebox. Starting price: about $300 to $600 setup, $150 to $300 a month. Be clear that you prepare; a qualified person still approves.

6. Weekly business report

Every Monday, the numbers the client cares about (sales, leads, site visits, unpaid invoices) become a one-page summary with three things worth their attention this week. Who buys it: owners who know they should look at their numbers and never do. Starting price: about $400 to $700 setup, $150 to $300 a month.

7. Lead research and CRM cleanup

Before each sales call, AI researches the prospect from public information and fills in missing CRM details; it also flags duplicates and stale contacts. Who buys it: anyone with a sales pipeline and a messy CRM. Starting price: about $300 to $600 setup, $150 to $350 a month.

8. Standard operating procedures

The client records a screen share or talks a task through; AI turns it into a clean, step-by-step procedure, and you build the library. Who buys it: owners about to hire, delegate or take a holiday. Starting price: about $75 to $200 per procedure, or a package of ten. A good way in, because the SOPs also show you where the next AI service is.

9. Newsletter drafting

AI drafts the weekly or monthly newsletter from the client's notes, recent posts and industry news; you edit, format and schedule it. Who buys it: anyone whose newsletter “goes out when we get round to it.” Starting price: about $300 to $500 setup, $200 to $500 a month.

10. New client onboarding pack

When the client signs a customer, AI drafts the welcome email, kickoff agenda, intake questions and first-week checklist, personalized from the signed proposal. Who buys it: agencies, consultants, coaches and service firms. Starting price: about $400 to $800 setup, $100 to $250 a month.

Which AI service should you sell first?

The one that answers a complaint you've already heard. If a client said “I'm drowning in email” last week, it's service 1. If they said “I never post anymore,” it's service 3. A service that fixes a pain the client has already named doesn't need much selling.

If you have no complaint to go on, start with meeting follow-up. It's quick to set up, easy to show, and nearly every client has meetings.

Should you sell services one at a time or as a package?

Start with one, prove it, then add. After the first month, the hours-saved number makes the second service an easy conversation. Once a client has two or three running, package them into a single monthly fee with a short monthly report. That's when you stop being “the VA” and become the person who runs their AI, which is a much harder role to replace. The approach is the same as in turning one-off clients into repeat work.

What shouldn't a VA sell as an AI service?

  • Anything regulated without a qualified person signing off: legal, tax, medical or financial advice.
  • Fully automatic sending to customers with no human check, at least until a workflow has months of clean results.
  • Work on data the client can't legally share with an AI tool, such as patient records without the right agreements in place.
  • “AI strategy” for a large company. Stick to concrete workflows you can build, run and measure.

How can AI help you build your services menu?

1

Match services to your clients

Prompt

I'm a virtual assistant adding AI services. Here are my current clients, with their business type, what I do for them and anything they've complained about recently: [list, without private details]. Here are ten services I could offer: inbox triage, meeting follow-up, content repurposing, support drafts, bookkeeping prep, weekly report, lead research and CRM cleanup, SOPs, newsletter drafting, onboarding packs. Step 1: For each client, rank the three services most likely to land, with one line on why, tied to what they've said. Step 2: Estimate hours saved per month for the top service for each client. Step 3: Tell me which single client and service to start with and why. Step 4: Write the one-sentence offer for it in the client's terms, not AI terms.

2

Write a one-page services menu

Prompt

Write a one-page AI services menu for my VA business. My clients are mostly [type of business]. Offer these services: [pick three to five from the list]. For each one write: a plain-English name (no jargon), the problem it fixes in one sentence, what the client gets each week or month, roughly how many hours it saves, and a starting setup and monthly price of [prices]. Add a short section on how I keep their data safe and that a person checks everything before it goes out. End with one simple next step. Keep it warm, direct and under 400 words. Don't use the words “revolutionize,” “leverage” or “seamless.”

You don't need all ten. One service, done well for one client, with a clear hours-saved number, is a business. The other nine are what you add next.

Frequently asked questions

What AI services can a virtual assistant offer?

Inbox triage and draft replies, meeting notes and follow-up, content repurposing, customer support first drafts, bookkeeping prep, a weekly business report, lead research and CRM cleanup, standard operating procedures, newsletter drafting, and new client onboarding packs.

What is the easiest AI service for a VA to start with?

Meeting notes and follow-up. It's quick to set up, nearly every client has meetings, and the before and after is obvious from a single call. Otherwise, start with whatever fixes a task the client has recently complained about.

How much can a VA charge for AI services?

Typical starting prices for US small-business clients are about $250 to $900 to set up a service and $100 to $600 a month to run it, depending on the service, the client's size and the hours it saves.

Do clients actually pay for AI services from a VA?

Yes, when the offer is framed as fixing a specific problem, such as a cluttered inbox or follow-ups that never go out, and backed by an hours-saved number. Clients pay for the result and for someone accountable, not for “AI.”

Should a VA sell AI services one at a time or as a package?

Start with one, prove the hours saved, then add a second. Once two or three are running, bundle them into one monthly fee with a short monthly report.

What AI services should a VA avoid selling?

Regulated advice such as legal, tax, medical or financial advice without a qualified person signing off, fully automatic sending to customers with no human check, work on data the client can't legally share with an AI tool, and vague “AI strategy” for large companies.

Do I need special tools to deliver these AI services?

Usually not. Most run on one AI assistant such as ChatGPT or Claude, a meeting recorder for transcripts, and the client's existing apps, with an automation tool like Zapier only when a workflow needs a trigger.
