How do virtual assistants keep client data safe when using AI?

The first question a careful client asks about AI is "what happens to my data?" If you can answer it in one calm sentence, and back it up with a one-page agreement, you win the work. Here's how to set that up.

By Denise Cullom · November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Work in the client's own AI account where you can, or in a business or team plan that doesn't use your chats to train its models, and check that setting yourself. Never ask for passwords: use delegated access, shared inboxes and app permissions you can remove. Share only what each task needs, and keep the highest-risk data (card numbers, ID numbers, passwords, health and legal records) out of AI tools entirely unless the client has a plan that covers it. Write the rules on one page the client signs, keep a simple log of what each AI system can reach, and review access every quarter.

Clients who hire a VA are already trusting you with their inbox, their calendar and often their customers. Adding AI to that work raises a fair question: where does all of this go? Most of the risk comes from a few avoidable habits, and a VA who has clear answers stands out from one who just says "it's fine."

This is a practical checklist, not legal advice. If a client works in a regulated field such as health, law or finance, ask what rules they already follow and work inside them.

Whose AI account should you use for client work?

The safest setup is the client's own account, on a business or team plan, with you added as a user. The data stays with them, they can see what's set up, and when the contract ends they remove you and keep everything. This is how the client kits are installed.

If you use your own account for several clients, use a paid business or team plan, check in its settings and data policy that your chats aren't used to train models, and keep each client in a separate project or workspace. Never mix two clients' files in one conversation. A free personal account is the wrong place for client data.

What should never go into an AI tool?

  • Passwords, security codes and recovery keys. Not even to "format a list".
  • Full card and bank numbers. The last four digits are enough for any admin task.
  • Government ID numbers such as Social Security or passport numbers.
  • Health, legal and HR records, unless the client's plan and agreements explicitly cover them.
  • Anything the client has marked confidential, such as a deal under negotiation or an unannounced hire.

For everything else, share the minimum the task needs. To draft a reply, the AI needs the email thread, not the whole inbox. To summarize a call, it needs the transcript, not the client's file share. When you can, remove names and replace them with roles ("the customer", "the supplier") before you paste.

How do you get access without asking for passwords?

Almost every tool a VA works in has a way to share access without sharing a password:

  • Email and calendar: delegated access, or a shared mailbox the client can switch off.
  • Documents: a shared folder for your work, not their whole drive.
  • Apps and AI connections: sign in with your own user on their account, and connect only the apps the task needs.
  • Passwords that have to be shared: a password manager's sharing feature, so you never see or store the password in plain text.

The test is simple: if the client wanted to remove your access tomorrow, could they do it in five minutes without changing their own password? If yes, the setup is right.

What goes in an AI data agreement with a client?

One page, in plain English, signed before you switch anything on:

  1. Which AI tools you use and whose account they run in.
  2. What data each system can see, task by task.
  3. What never goes in, using the list above plus anything they add.
  4. Who reviews the output before it reaches a customer. For anything sent in the client's name, that's you, every time, until they agree otherwise.
  5. How access is removed when the work ends, and what happens to any files you hold.
  6. What you'll do if something goes wrong, and how fast you'll tell them.

Clients rarely push back on this. Most are relieved someone has thought about it, and it often becomes the reason they choose you over a cheaper VA.

What should you do if something goes wrong?

Mistakes happen: a draft goes to the wrong person, a file lands in the wrong client's project, an AI connection can see more than it should. The rule is to tell the client the same day, say what happened and what you've done about it, then fix the cause. Remove the access, delete the misplaced file, tighten the setting, and write the fix into your process. Clients forgive mistakes handled openly. They don't forgive finding out later.

How do you keep it safe over time?

Keep a short access log per client: each AI system, what it connects to, and when you last checked it. Every quarter, spend fifteen minutes on it. Remove connections you no longer use, check the training setting is still off, and confirm the client still wants each system running. Add a line about it to your monthly results report so the client sees the care you're taking.

Which prompts should you use?

The first strips personal details before you paste something in:

1

Remove personal details first

Prompt

Before I work on the text below, replace every person's name with their role (the customer, the supplier, the manager), and replace email addresses, phone numbers, street addresses and account numbers with [removed]. Keep everything else exactly as it is. Then list what you replaced, by role only, so I can check nothing was missed. Text: [paste]

The second drafts your one-page agreement:

2

Draft a one-page AI data agreement

Prompt

I'm a virtual assistant about to set up AI systems for a client. Tools: [list]. They run in [the client's account / my business account]. The systems are: [list each task and the data it needs]. Step 1: Write a one-page agreement in plain English with these sections: tools and accounts, data each system can see, what never goes in, who reviews output, removing access when the work ends, what happens if something goes wrong. Step 2: Add a never-goes-in list starting with passwords, full card and bank numbers, ID numbers, and health, legal and HR records. Step 3: Keep it friendly, not legalistic, and under 400 words. Step 4: End with a line for both of us to sign and date.

The third runs your quarterly check:

3

Run the quarterly access review

Prompt

Here is my access log for a client: [paste: each AI system, what it connects to, date last checked]. Step 1: For each line, list the questions I should answer: is it still in use, does it need every connection it has, is the training setting still off, does the client still want it. Step 2: Flag any system that hasn't been checked in over 90 days. Step 3: Write a three-line note for the client's monthly report summarizing the review. Don't assume anything that isn't in my log.

Write your one-page agreement once, then reuse it for every client. It turns "is this safe?" from a reason to say no into a reason to hire you.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe for a VA to use ChatGPT or Claude with client data?

It can be, with the right setup: use the client's own business account or a paid business plan that doesn't train on your chats, share only what each task needs, keep passwords, card numbers, ID numbers and sensitive records out, and agree the rules with the client in writing.

Should a VA use their own AI account or the client's?

The client's own account is safest, with you added as a user, because the data stays with them and they can remove your access at any time. If you use your own, use a paid business or team plan, keep each client in a separate project, and never mix clients in one conversation.

What should never be put into an AI tool?

Passwords and security codes, full card and bank numbers, government ID numbers, health, legal and HR records unless the client's plan covers them, and anything the client has marked confidential.

How do VAs get access to client accounts safely?

Use delegated email and calendar access, shared folders, your own user on the client's apps and a password manager's sharing feature. Never ask for a client's password. The client should be able to remove your access in minutes without changing their own password.

What should a VA's AI data agreement include?

Which tools you use and whose account they run in, what data each system can see, what never goes in, who reviews output, how access is removed when the work ends, and what you'll do if something goes wrong. Keep it to one page in plain English.

What should a VA do after an AI data mistake?

Tell the client the same day, explain what happened and what you've done, then fix the cause: remove the access, delete the misplaced file, tighten the setting, and add the fix to your process.
