How do you follow up with a prospect who stopped replying?

Silence usually isn't a no. It's a busy person who needs a better reason to reply. Here's how many times to follow up, when, what to say, and the AI prompts that write the whole sequence in your own voice.

By Denise Cullom · October 19, 2026 · 9 min read

The short answer

Follow up four or five times over about four weeks, then stop. Make every follow-up give something (a useful idea, a relevant resource, or a short client story) instead of just “checking in,” and end each one with an easy question they can answer in a few words. Time it to the situation: three to five business days after a first message, the same day after a call, and day 2, 5 and 10 after a proposal. Finish with a polite close that leaves the door open, then move them to a nurture list. AI can draft the whole sequence in your own voice in a few minutes, so the only thing left for you is to send it on time.

Here's the part of getting clients that nobody likes talking about. You send a good message, or have a great call, or write a proposal you're proud of, and then nothing. No reply. Most freelancers wait a week, decide they've been rejected, and quietly move on.

That's where a lot of clients get lost. In my years running sales and operations teams, the deals that closed were rarely the ones where the first message landed perfectly. They were the ones where someone followed up well, on time, without making the buyer feel chased. The good news is that this is a system, not a personality trait, and AI makes the system almost effortless.

Why do prospects stop replying?

Silence almost never means “no.” Usually it means one of these:

  • They got busy. Your message arrived on a bad day and sank under fifty others.
  • It isn't urgent yet. They're interested, but the problem you solve isn't the fire they're fighting this week.
  • They need someone else's OK. A partner, a boss or a budget has to agree, and they haven't asked yet.
  • They're unsure what to say. They aren't ready to say yes and feel awkward saying no, so they say nothing.
  • Your ask was too big. “Let me know your thoughts on the proposal” takes effort to answer. “Would March or April be a better start?” doesn't.

Every one of these is fixed by a short, useful, well-timed follow-up. None is fixed by waiting.

How many times should you follow up?

Plan for four or five touches in total: your first message, three or four follow-ups spread over about four weeks, and a polite close at the end. Space them out so each one has room to breathe, for example day 0, day 4, day 10, day 18 and day 28.

Fewer than that and you give up just as many buyers are getting around to replying. More than that, in a short window, and you start to feel like pressure. After the close, stop direct follow-ups. That person isn't lost. They move to a nurture list you contact every two or three months with something genuinely useful, and a surprising number come back when the timing is right.

When should you follow up in each situation?

The timing depends on how far the conversation got. A good default for each case:

  • No reply to a first message: follow up after three to five business days, then run the rest of the sequence.
  • After a discovery call: send a short recap the same day with the next step you agreed on. If they go quiet, follow up after about a week.
  • After sending a proposal: follow up on day 2 (“any questions?”), day 5 (offer a quick call or a smaller option) and day 10 (ask for a clear yes, no or not yet).
  • They missed a booked call: reach out the same day, with no guilt, and offer two new times or your booking link.
  • They said “not right now”: thank them, ask when would be a better time, and put that date in your tracker.

What should a follow-up message say?

One rule covers almost everything: every follow-up should give something, not just ask for something. “Just bumping this to the top of your inbox” gives nothing. A useful idea gives them a reason to reply. Rotate through four kinds of message:

  1. The useful idea. One practical tip they can use today, connected to the problem you solve. No ask, or a very small one.
  2. The resource. An article, a checklist or a short video that fits their situation, with one sentence on why you thought of them.
  3. The short story. Two or three sentences about how you helped someone similar get a specific result, ending with an easy question.
  4. The polite close. You say you'll stop reaching out, remove all pressure, and leave the door open.

Here's what that looks like for a virtual assistant who messaged a busy real estate agent:

Touch 2, the useful idea: “Hi Maria, one thing that saves my agents hours: a saved reply for every common buyer question (showings, offers, closing costs). Set up ten of them and most of your inbox writes itself. Happy to share my list if it helps.”

Touch 4, the short story: “Hi Maria, a client of mine was answering listing inquiries at 10pm every night. We set up a same-hour reply system and she got her evenings back in two weeks. Is evening admin a problem for you too, or is your bigger headache somewhere else?”

Touch 5, the polite close: “Hi Maria, I don't want to crowd your inbox, so this is my last note for now. If getting admin off your plate becomes a priority, just reply here and I'll pick it up. Wishing you a great spring market.”

How do you use AI to write follow-ups?

AI is perfect for follow-ups because the hard part isn't the writing, it's thinking of something fresh and useful to say each time. Give it your context once and it will draft the whole sequence. Two things make the difference between a draft that sounds like you and one that sounds like a robot: your voice rules (a few of your own messages, plus the phrases you never use) and the real situation (what you sent, when, and what this person cares about).

Start with this prompt when a first message has gone unanswered:

1

Write a full follow-up sequence

Prompt

You are helping me follow up with a prospect without being pushy. I am a [your role] who helps [type of client] with [result you deliver]. Here are two messages I've written myself so you can match my voice: [paste two short messages]. Never use these phrases: “just checking in,” “circling back,” “touch base,” “did you see my last email.” Here is the first message I sent to [name, role and company] on [date], with no reply: [paste message]. What I know about them: [one or two details from their website, posts or our conversation]. Write four follow-ups to send over the next four weeks, and suggest a send day for each. Follow-up 1: one practical tip they can use today, no ask. Follow-up 2: share [resource or topic] and say in one sentence why it fits them. Follow-up 3: a short story about a result for a similar client (leave a placeholder for my real example) ending with an easy question. Follow-up 4: a polite close that says I'll stop reaching out and leaves the door open. Each under 60 words. No guilt, no fake urgency, no exclamation marks.

When the silence comes later, after a call or a proposal, the follow-up needs to bring back what the prospect was excited about and make the next step tiny:

2

Restart a conversation after a call or proposal

Prompt

You are helping me restart a stalled sales conversation. On [date] I had a call with [name] at [company] about [their problem]. They said their main goal was [goal, in their words if possible] and they were worried about [concern]. On [date] I sent a proposal for [offer] at [price]. I haven't heard back in [number] days. Here's my voice: [paste one or two messages I wrote]. Write three short follow-ups for day 2, day 5 and day 10 after the proposal. Day 2: ask if any questions came up, and remind them of the one result they cared most about. Day 5: offer a 10-minute call to walk through it, or a smaller first step if budget or timing is the issue. Day 10: ask for a simple answer, yes, no or not yet, and make it clear that any answer is fine. Each under 50 words, ending with a question they can answer in one line.

Always read the draft before you send it and add one detail only you would know: something they said on the call, a post they wrote, a name you both know. That one line is what makes a follow-up feel personal instead of automated.

What should you never say in a follow-up?

  • “Just checking in” or “bumping this up.” It asks for their time and gives nothing back.
  • “Did you get my last email?” They did. This makes them feel caught out.
  • Guilt. “I've reached out several times now” turns a busy person into a guilty one, and guilty people avoid you.
  • Fake urgency. Invented deadlines and “only two spots left” erode trust the moment they're noticed.
  • The whole pitch again. They read it the first time. Say one new thing, not the same thing louder.
  • A big ask. “Thoughts?” is hard to answer. “Would next Tuesday or Thursday work?” is easy.

How do you keep track of who to follow up with?

Follow-up fails when it lives in your head. Keep a simple tracker, a spreadsheet is enough, with one row per prospect and three columns that matter most: last contact, next step and next date. Every time you send a message, fill in the next date before you close the tab.

Then give it ten minutes once a week. Sort by next date, send everything that's due, and let AI help you decide where to spend your energy:

3

Plan this week's follow-ups

Prompt

You are my sales assistant. Here is my prospect list, with the last time I contacted each person, what I sent, where the conversation stands and any notes: [paste your tracker rows]. Today is [date]. Step 1: List everyone who is due or overdue for a follow-up, most important first, and say why each one is a priority. Step 2: For each of them, say which kind of follow-up fits best (useful idea, resource, short story, proposal check-in or polite close), based on what they've already received. Step 3: Flag anyone who has had five or more touches with no reply, so I can send a polite close and move them to my nurture list. Step 4: Draft the top three messages, under 60 words each, in a friendly, direct voice.

When should you stop following up?

Stop direct follow-up after the polite close, or sooner if they ask you to. If they say no, thank them, ask one short question about why (budget, timing, fit), and use the answer to improve your offer. If they say “not now,” ask when to check back and put that date in your tracker.

Everyone else goes on your nurture list. Every two or three months, send them something genuinely useful with no pitch attached: a tip, an article, a quick congratulations on a win you saw. You stay the person they think of when the timing finally lines up, and you never had to chase anyone to get there.

Frequently asked questions

How many times should you follow up with a prospect before giving up?

Four or five touches in total is a good rule for a cold or warm prospect: your first message plus three or four follow-ups spread over about four weeks, ending with a polite close. After that, stop direct follow-ups and move them to a nurture list you contact every two to three months with something genuinely useful.

How long should you wait before following up?

For a first message with no reply, wait three to five business days. After a discovery call, send a recap the same day and follow up after about a week if they go quiet. After a proposal, follow up on day 2, day 5 and day 10. After a missed call, reach out the same day with new times.

What should you say when a prospect goes silent?

Give them a reason to reply that isn't guilt. Share one useful idea, a relevant resource, or a short story about a result for a similar client, and end with an easy question they can answer in a few words. Avoid “just checking in,” “did you see my last email” and fake deadlines.

Is it rude to follow up after a proposal?

No. Most buyers expect it, and many proposals stall because the person got busy, not because they said no. Keep it short, make it easy to answer, and offer to adjust the scope or timing. A clear yes or no is better for both of you than silence.

Can AI write follow-up emails that don't sound like AI?

Yes, if you give it your voice rules and real context. Paste a few messages you've written yourself, list the phrases you never use, and tell it the exact situation: what you sent, when, and what the prospect cares about. Then edit the draft so every message has one detail only you would know.

What is a breakup email in sales?

A breakup email, or polite close, is the last follow-up in a sequence. It says you won't keep reaching out, removes all pressure and leaves the door open. It often gets more replies than the earlier touches, because it gives the prospect an easy way to say “not now” or “yes, let's talk.”
