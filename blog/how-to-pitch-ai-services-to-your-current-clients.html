How do you pitch AI services to the clients you already work for?

The fastest first AI client isn't a new one. It's someone who already pays you and already trusts you. Here's how to bring AI up so it sounds like help, not a sales pitch, and how to turn it into a raise.

By Denise Cullom · November 5, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

Start with a client who already trusts you and one task that visibly eats their time, such as the inbox, meeting notes or monthly reports. Don't pitch "AI". Pitch the result: "I can get your inbox down from an hour a day to fifteen minutes of your time." Offer a two-week trial on that one task, measure the time before and after, and share the number. Then propose a one-time setup fee plus a monthly amount on top of your normal invoice. Answer the safety question before they ask it, by explaining what the AI will and won't see and that you check everything before it goes out.

Most virtual assistants who want to offer AI services start by thinking about new clients: a new website, a new offer, a new audience. That's the slow road. The quick one is sitting in your inbox already, in the form of clients who pay you every month and would happily pay a bit more to get time back.

They know your work. They trust you with their email and their calendar. They don't need to be convinced you're good. All you need to show them is that something specific can get faster, and that you'll handle it safely.

Why are current clients the easiest people to sell AI services to?

  • Trust is already there. The biggest worry about AI is "what happens to my information?" A stranger has to earn that trust. You already have it.
  • You can see the problem. You know which tasks take them the longest because you do some of them yourself.
  • There's no sales process. No proposals to strangers, no discovery calls with people who might never reply. One conversation with someone you talk to every week.
  • Results show up fast. You can measure the before and after on their own work within two weeks.

Which task should you pitch first?

Pick the one that scores well on three things:

  1. It's repetitive. It happens daily or weekly, in roughly the same way each time.
  2. The client feels it. They complain about it, or it lands on their desk rather than yours.
  3. It's low risk. A mistake is easy to catch before it matters. Drafts, summaries and reports are low risk; payments and legal documents are not.

Good first candidates: inbox sorting and draft replies (see how VAs use AI for inbox and calendar management), meeting notes with action items, weekly reports, social posts from content they already have, and chasing unpaid invoices. Pick one. Pitching five things at once sounds like a sales pitch; pitching one sounds like a good idea.

What should you actually say?

Keep it short, lead with their problem, and don't use the word "AI" in the first sentence. Something like:

  • "I've noticed your inbox is taking you about an hour a day even with me sorting it."
  • "I've been setting up an AI system that sorts email, drafts replies in your voice and sends you a five-line summary each morning. You'd only review the drafts and make the decisions."
  • "I'd like to try it on your inbox for two weeks at no extra cost, and track how much time it saves you. If it works, we can talk about adding it properly."
  • "Nothing would go out without me checking it first, and I'd only use it on an account set up for your work."

Four sentences: the problem, the result, the trial and the safety point. That's the whole pitch. Send it in writing, or say it at the end of a regular check-in call and follow up in writing.

How do you run the two-week trial?

  1. Measure the before. For three or four days, note how long the task takes the client and you. Rough numbers are fine.
  2. Agree the rules in writing. What the AI will see, what it won't, which account it runs on, and that you review everything.
  3. Run it daily. Same time every day. Correct the AI's mistakes as you go, so week two is better than week one.
  4. Measure the after. Same task, same rough timing, at the end of week two.
  5. Share a one-page summary. Hours before, hours after, what that's worth at their hourly value, and one or two things that got better beyond time, like nothing slipping through the cracks.

The trial is free on purpose. It's two weeks of your time, and it buys you a number the client found themselves, which is far more persuasive than anything you could claim.

How do you answer the common objections?

  • "Is my information safe?" Explain the account setup, that training on their data is switched off where the plan allows, what you will never paste in (bank details, passwords, medical or HR matters), and that you check every output. Offer to put it in writing.
  • "Will it sound like a robot?" Show one draft written from their own past emails, next to an email they wrote. Most clients can't tell which is which.
  • "Does this mean I need you less?" No. It means the same hours go further. You'll spend less time on sorting and more on the work they'd like to hand over but can't yet.
  • "I can just use ChatGPT myself." They can, and most never do, because setting it up, writing the instructions and checking the results takes time. That's the service.

How do you price it after the trial?

Two numbers: a one-time setup fee for building the system (the instructions, the rules, the testing), and a monthly amount for running and improving it. A common starting point for the monthly figure is about a quarter of the time value it saves the client. If the system saves 10 hours a month for a client whose time is worth $60 an hour, that's $600 of value, so about $150 a month is easy to justify, on top of your existing hours.

Which prompts help you prepare the pitch?

Use the first to choose which task to pitch:

1

Find the best task to pitch

Prompt

I'm a virtual assistant. Here's what I do for this client each week and roughly how long each task takes, for me and for them: [list tasks and times]. Their business: [one sentence]. Step 1: Score each task from 1 to 5 on how repetitive it is, how much the client feels it, and how low risk it is if AI makes a mistake. Step 2: Rank them by total score. Step 3: For the top two, explain in two lines how AI could help and what I'd still do myself. Step 4: Recommend one to pitch first and say why.

The second drafts the pitch message in your own voice:

2

Write the pitch message

Prompt

Write a short message from me to a client I've worked with for [time]. The task I want to improve: [task], which takes them about [time] a week. My plan: [what the AI system would do and what I'd still check]. Step 1: Open with their problem, not with AI. Step 2: Describe the result in plain words and numbers. Step 3: Offer a free two-week trial and say how I'll measure it. Step 4: Add one sentence on how their information stays safe. Keep it under 120 words, warm and direct, in my voice: [paste two messages I've sent them].

The third turns the trial into the one-page results summary:

3

Write the trial results summary

Prompt

Here are my before and after notes from a two-week AI trial on my client's [task]: [paste timings and notes]. Their time is worth about [$ per hour]. Step 1: Work out the hours saved per week and per month, and the monthly value. Step 2: List up to three improvements beyond time, using only what's in my notes. Step 3: Write a one-page summary the client can read in a minute, with the numbers first. Step 4: End with a short proposal: a setup fee of [$] and [$] a month to keep it running. Don't exaggerate anything.

One client, one task, two weeks. Do it once and you'll have a real result to talk about with every other client, and the confidence to pitch it to new ones.

Frequently asked questions

How do I offer AI services to my existing VA clients?

Pick one repetitive task the client feels, such as their inbox or meeting notes, and offer a free two-week trial on just that task. Measure the time it takes before and after, share the result in a one-page summary, then propose a setup fee and a monthly amount.

Should I mention AI when pitching a client?

Lead with the result instead: less time in the inbox, notes ready after every call, reports done on time. Then explain that you use an AI system to do it, that you check everything, and how their information stays safe.

How much should I charge an existing client for AI services?

A common approach is a one-time setup fee for building the system plus a monthly amount of roughly a quarter of the time value it saves them. If it saves 10 hours a month at $60 an hour, about $150 a month on top of your normal hours is reasonable.

What if my client worries about data safety?

Explain which account the AI runs on, that training on their data is turned off where the plan allows, what you will never paste in, and that nothing goes out without your review. Offer to put those rules in writing before you start.

Will offering AI services mean my client needs fewer hours from me?

Usually not. The time saved on routine tasks goes into work the client wanted to hand over but couldn't. Pricing the AI service separately, as a setup fee and monthly amount, also keeps your income from shrinking as you get faster.

Should the trial be free?

A short free trial on one task works well with existing clients, because it costs you a couple of weeks and gives you a result measured on their own work. Keep it to one task and a fixed end date so it doesn't become free work indefinitely.
