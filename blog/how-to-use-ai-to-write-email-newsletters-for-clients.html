How do virtual assistants use AI to write email newsletters for clients?

Most small businesses know they should email their list. Most don't, because nobody has time to write it. That makes a regular newsletter one of the easiest AI services for a VA to take off a client's plate, as long as it still sounds like them and says something real.

By Denise Cullom · November 16, 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer

Write a one-page newsletter brief with the client first: who reads it, what it's for, how often it goes out, the sections, and the one action each issue asks for. Each month, collect real material from the client's work: questions customers asked, new offers, events, wins, a tip they shared. Give AI the brief, the material and two past emails in the client's voice, and ask for a draft. Then edit for voice, check every link, date and price, send a test, and get the client's approval before it goes out. After each send, report opens, clicks and replies in three lines.

A newsletter is a small job with a big payoff for the client. It keeps them in front of past customers, it brings in repeat business, and it's the one marketing channel they own outright. The hard part was never the sending. It was writing something every month. That's the part AI speeds up, and the part where a VA adds the judgment AI doesn't have.

What goes in a newsletter brief?

Before the first issue, sit down with the client for twenty minutes and write a one-page brief. You'll reuse it every month.

  1. The readers: past customers, leads, members, local clients.
  2. The goal: repeat bookings, event sign-ups, referrals, keeping in touch.
  3. How often: monthly is a realistic start. Weekly only if there's real material.
  4. The sections: for example, one useful tip, one piece of news, one offer or event.
  5. The one action each issue asks for: book, reply, register, refer.
  6. The voice: two or three past emails the client wrote that sound like them, and any words they never use.
  7. Who approves and how much notice they need.

If you already keep a client rulebook (see onboarding a new client), add the newsletter brief to it.

Where does the material come from?

A newsletter written from nothing reads like it. Keep a shared folder or note for the month and drop things into it as they happen:

  • Questions customers asked by email or phone. Each one is a tip waiting to be written.
  • New products, services, prices or opening hours.
  • Events, deadlines and seasonal reminders.
  • Something the client said on a call that was worth sharing.
  • A customer win, but only with the customer's permission, and only what actually happened.

If you handle the client's inbox (see AI for inbox management), you'll see most of this anyway. Two minutes a week saving it is enough.

How do you get AI to draft it in the client's voice?

Give the AI the brief, the month's material and the sample emails together, and ask for one draft that follows the sections. Ask for three subject lines, not one. Then edit. Read it out loud and cut anything the client wouldn't say. Swap general claims for specifics from the material. If a sentence could appear in anyone's newsletter, it probably shouldn't be in this one. The guide to using AI without sounding like AI covers the words to watch for.

One rule matters more than the rest: AI doesn't add facts. Every price, date, statistic and promise in the email must come from the client's material. If the draft includes something you can't trace back, take it out.

What do you check before it goes out?

  • Every link opens the right page.
  • Every date, time and price matches the client's material.
  • The one action is clear and the button works.
  • The footer has the unsubscribe link and the business mailing address, which anti-spam laws such as CAN-SPAM in the US require.
  • The right list or segment is selected.
  • A test email looks right on a phone.
  • The client approved the final version, in writing.

Send from the client's own email platform account, with your own login if it allows team members. Don't paste the client's subscriber list into an AI tool. The draft doesn't need it. The rules in keeping client data safe apply here too.

How do you report on a newsletter?

Three lines after each send is enough: how many people opened it, how many clicked the main link, and any replies or bookings that came from it. Note one thing to try next time, such as a different subject line style or a shorter email. Over a few months this shows the client what the newsletter is doing for them. Add it to your monthly report (see showing clients the time AI saves).

How do you price a newsletter service?

Price it as a monthly package: a setup fee for the brief, template and first issue, then a monthly fee covering material collection, drafting, editing, sending and the report. The guide to charging for AI work walks through the numbers. Newsletters pair well with social media management, since the same monthly material feeds both.

Which prompts should you use?

The first builds the brief from your kickoff notes:

1

Write the newsletter brief

Prompt

From my notes on a call with my client, write a one-page newsletter brief. Notes: [paste notes]. Sections: readers, goal, how often, the sections of each issue, the one action each issue asks for, voice (with phrases they use and words they never use), who approves and how much notice they need. Mark anything my notes don't answer so I can ask the client.

The second drafts the issue:

2

Draft this month's newsletter

Prompt

Write this month's newsletter for my client. Brief: [paste brief]. This month's material: [paste notes, questions, news, offers]. Two past emails in their voice: [paste]. Follow the sections in the brief, ask for the one action once and clearly, and keep it under 350 words. Use only facts from the material. Don't invent prices, dates, results or quotes. Give me three subject lines under 50 characters and a one-line preview text.

The third checks the draft before you send it to the client:

3

Check the draft against the material

Prompt

Compare this newsletter draft with the source material. Draft: [paste]. Material: [paste]. Step 1: List every fact, date, price and claim in the draft and say whether it appears in the material. Step 2: Flag anything that doesn't. Step 3: Point out sentences that sound generic or unlike the sample emails. Step 4: Confirm the one action is clear. Don't rewrite the email. Just give me the list.

The first three issues take the longest, while you learn the client's voice. After that, a monthly newsletter usually becomes one of the steadiest jobs on your list.

Frequently asked questions

Can a virtual assistant write a client's newsletter with AI?

Yes. The VA writes a one-page newsletter brief with the client, collects real material during the month, has AI draft the issue in the client's voice from past emails, then edits it, checks every link, date and price, and gets the client's approval before sending.

How do you make an AI-written newsletter sound like the client?

Give the AI two or three emails the client actually wrote, plus a list of words they never use, and build each issue from things that happened in their business that month. Then read it out loud and cut anything the client wouldn't say.

What should be checked before sending a newsletter?

Every link, date and price, the main button, the footer with the unsubscribe link and the business mailing address, the selected list, a test email on a phone, and the client's written approval of the final version.

Should you paste a subscriber list into an AI tool?

No. Drafting the newsletter doesn't need the list. Keep subscriber data in the client's email platform and send from there.

How often should a small business send a newsletter?

Monthly is a realistic start for most small businesses. Send more often only if there is enough real material to make each issue useful.
