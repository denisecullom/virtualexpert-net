How do you choose a niche and write an offer clients say yes to?

“I help anyone with anything” is the hardest thing to sell. Here's how to pick a niche from work you've already done, say what you do in one sentence, and turn it into an offer with a price.

By Denise Cullom · October 27, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer

Pick the niche from work you've already done, not from a list of trendy industries. Look at your best past clients and find the overlap: who they were, what problem you solved, and whether you can find more people like them. Then name the problem in their words, not yours, and write one sentence: I help [who] [get what result] [how]. Turn it into one first offer with a named result, a timeline and a price, so buying is a simple decision. Test it on ten real conversations before you redo your website, and remember a niche describes who you market to, not the only person you're allowed to work with.

Every freelancer hears “you need to niche down,” and most hate the advice. It sounds like turning away work you need. But the reason to narrow isn't to earn less. It's that a stranger decides in seconds whether you're for them, and “I do marketing” gives them nothing to decide with.

In the companies I ran, we never hired the generalist agency. We hired the one whose website seemed to describe our exact problem. They weren't more talented. They were just easier to recognize.

What does a niche actually mean?

A niche is a group of people with the same problem that you can reach in a repeatable way. It usually has two parts: who (bookkeepers, dental practices, newly funded startups, wedding photographers) and what problem (invoices going out late, no follow-up after enquiries, a launch with nobody to run it).

Either part alone is weak. “I work with coaches” is broad enough to mean nothing. “I help coaches who've just launched a group program keep up with the admin and the inbox” makes a particular person sit up.

How do you choose your niche?

Start from evidence instead of imagination. Four steps:

  1. List your best past clients or projects. Include jobs, volunteer work and favors, not just paid freelance work. For each one, write down the kind of business, what you did and what changed for them.
  2. Mark the ones you enjoyed and did well. You're looking for the overlap of “I was good at this” and “I'd happily do it again.”
  3. Find the pattern. It might be an industry, a size of business, a moment (just hired their first employee, just launched, just lost an assistant), or a type of problem.
  4. Check you can find more of them. Is there somewhere these people gather, a list you can build, a group, a conference, a search you can run? A niche you can't reach isn't a niche, it's a wish.

If you're brand new and have no past clients, use the work you've done inside jobs. The eight years you spent in a dental office is a niche. So is the department you ran, or the software you know inside out.

How narrow should a niche be?

Narrow enough that your ideal client thinks “that's me,” and wide enough that there are hundreds of them you can reach. For most solo businesses that's something like “small accounting firms” rather than “small businesses,” and rarely as narrow as “accounting firms in one town with exactly four staff.”

Remember what a niche is for: it decides your marketing, not your morals. If someone outside it shows up with a project you want, take it. The niche is simply who your website, your posts and your outreach are written for.

How do you name the problem in their words?

This is where most positioning goes wrong. We describe our service (“inbox management,” “revenue operations,” “brand strategy”) and the client describes their pain (“I'm answering emails at 11pm,” “we have no idea which deals are real,” “our stuff looks homemade”). Sell to the second one.

Where to find their words: review sites, forum posts and community threads, questions in Facebook or Slack groups, the exact phrases past clients used on your calls, and comments on posts about your topic. Collect ten real sentences. Those are your headlines.

What is a positioning sentence?

One line you can say on a call or put at the top of your website:

I help [who] [get this result] [without this pain, or in this timeframe].

Some examples:

  • “I help small accounting firms get their client onboarding down from three weeks to three days.”
  • “I help coaches running group programs hand over the inbox and calendar in their first two weeks.”
  • “I help dental practices stop losing new-patient enquiries that come in after hours.”

Say it out loud. If you wince, it's too clever or too vague. The test is whether a stranger can repeat it back to someone else, because that's how referrals travel.

How do you turn a niche into an offer?

A niche tells people who you're for. An offer gives them something to buy. Give it four things:

  1. A name that says what it does, like “Inbox and calendar handover” or “30-day content reset.”
  2. A result the client can picture, not a list of tasks.
  3. A timeline, so they know when life gets better.
  4. A price, ideally fixed for a clear first step, as in how to price your services.

Start with one offer. One clear thing you're known for beats five services nobody can keep straight. You can add the next one once this one sells.

How do you test a niche before you commit?

Don't rebuild your website first. Test it in conversations, which is faster and cheaper:

  • Ten conversations. Use your sentence on ten people who fit: in messages, on calls, in groups. Watch for “how do you do that?” versus a polite nod.
  • Listen for their words. If they explain your own offer back to you in better language, use theirs.
  • Count the questions about price. People who ask what it costs are telling you the problem is real.
  • Give it a few weeks. Changing your niche every fortnight is the same as having none. Only move on if nobody recognizes the problem after a genuine run at it.

How can AI help you find your niche?

AI is useful here as a pattern-spotter and an editor, not an oracle. It doesn't know your market, but it's very good at finding the overlap in a messy list and turning it into plain sentences. Start by dumping your history into it:

1

Find the pattern in your past work

Prompt

Here is a list of my past clients, jobs and projects, with what I did and what changed for them: [paste 8 to 15 of them, messy is fine]. I enjoyed these most: [list]. Step 1: Group them and tell me what the strongest two or three patterns are, by industry, by business size, by the moment the client was in, and by the type of problem I solved. Step 2: For each pattern, write the client in one sentence and the problem in the words that client would use, not industry jargon. Step 3: Say which pattern looks most promising for a solo business and what you'd need to know to be sure. Ask me up to five questions before you answer if it would make the answer better.

Then sharpen the sentence, with hard limits so it stays plain:

2

Write and sharpen your one sentence

Prompt

My niche is [who] and the problem I solve is [problem, in their words]. My voice rules: [paste]. Step 1: Write eight versions of a positioning sentence in the form "I help [who] [get this result] [without this pain or in this timeframe]." No buzzwords, no "empower," "unlock," "seamless" or "transform," under 20 words each. Step 2: For each, say what a stranger would assume I do and who would feel left out. Step 3: Pick the two strongest and tell me why. Step 4: Rewrite the best one as a website headline, a LinkedIn headline under 220 characters, and the first line I'd say when someone asks what I do.

And package the offer so there's something concrete to buy:

3

Package your first offer

Prompt

I help [one sentence]. The work usually involves [describe what you actually do] and takes about [time]. Step 1: Suggest three names for this as a packaged offer, each saying what it does in plain words. Step 2: For the best one, write the result the client gets, what's included and what isn't, the timeline, and what I'd need from them. Step 3: Suggest a sensible small first step I could sell on its own, and what would naturally come after it. Step 4: List the three objections a [type of client] would raise about this offer and an honest answer to each. Keep it to one page.

What mistakes keep people stuck?

  • Picking a niche you've never worked in because it looks lucrative. You'll have no stories, no vocabulary and no network there.
  • Describing the service instead of the result. Buyers don't want consulting, they want the thing consulting gives them.
  • Waiting until it's perfect. Ten conversations teach you more than a month of thinking.
  • Changing it every month. Nobody can refer you if they can't remember what you do.
  • Thinking a niche turns work away. It only decides who you speak to first.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose a niche as a freelancer?

Start from your own past work. List the clients and projects you enjoyed and did well, find the pattern in who they were and what problem you solved, and check you can find more people like them. A niche you can't reach isn't useful, however appealing it sounds.

How narrow should my niche be?

Narrow enough that your ideal client reads it and thinks “that's me,” wide enough that there are hundreds of them you can reach. For most solo businesses that means a specific type and size of business with a specific problem, not an entire market and not a single town.

Does niching down mean turning away other work?

No. A niche decides who your website, posts and outreach speak to. If someone outside it comes to you with work you want, you can still take it.

What is a positioning statement?

One sentence in the form “I help [who] [get this result] [without this pain or in this timeframe].” It should be plain enough that a stranger can repeat it to someone else, because that is how referrals travel.

What makes a good offer?

A name that says what it does, a result the client can picture, a timeline, and a price. Start with one offer and a small first step rather than a long list of services.

How do I test a niche before committing?

Use your one sentence in ten real conversations with people who fit, and watch whether they ask how you do it or just nod politely. Listen for the words they use and how often price comes up, and give it a few weeks before you judge it.

Can AI help me pick a niche?

AI can spot patterns in a list of your past clients, turn them into plain sentences and draft your offer, but it doesn't know your market. Use it to think faster, then test the result in real conversations.
