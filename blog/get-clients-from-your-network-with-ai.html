How to get your next client from people who already know you

Before you send one more cold message, work the list of people who already trust you. Here's a step-by-step plan, with AI doing the legwork, that takes about an hour to start and 15 minutes a week to keep going.

By Denise Cullom · October 10, 2026 · 9 min read

When freelancers, VAs, consultants and coaches tell me they need more clients, the first thing they usually reach for is cold outreach: a new list, a new LinkedIn campaign, a new template. Cold outreach works, and I teach it. But it's the slowest way to fill a calendar when you're starting from a standstill.

The fastest way is almost always sitting in your inbox and phone already. Past clients, former colleagues, people you met in a mastermind, the agency owner you referred work to two years ago. They know your work, they know you're good, and they don't need to be convinced you're real.

The problem isn't that these people won't help. It's that you haven't asked, because reaching out feels awkward and you don't know what to say. That's the exact part AI is good at.

Why your warm network beats cold outreach

When I was a COO helping scale a company from $5M to $16M, the cheapest, highest-converting revenue we ever had came from existing relationships: repeat buyers, upgrades and referrals. The same is true for a business of one, only more so. A stranger has to decide whether you're trustworthy, competent and worth the risk. Someone who has worked with you has already answered all three.

  • Replies come faster. People open messages from names they recognize.
  • Sales cycles are shorter. There's no “let me check out your website” phase.
  • Referrals arrive pre-sold. “You should talk to Sam, she was great” does more than any portfolio.

The catch is that it only works if you do it on purpose, consistently, and in a way that doesn't feel like begging. Here's the system.

Step 1: Build your “Warm 50” in 20 minutes

Open a spreadsheet (or the pipeline tracker if you have it) and list 50 people who would recognize your name. Don't filter yet. Pull from:

  • Past and current clients, including small one-off projects
  • Former coworkers, managers and people you managed
  • Your LinkedIn connections you've actually spoken to
  • Your email “sent” folder from the last two years
  • People from groups, courses, masterminds and communities you've been part of
  • Other service providers who sell to the same clients you do (your future referral partners)

For each person, add one line of context: how you know them and anything you remember about their work. That one line is what will let AI write something personal instead of generic.

Then let AI sort the list for you.

1

Sort your Warm 50

Prompt

I'm a [your role] who helps [who you help] with [result]. Below is a list of people I know, with one line of context on each. Sort them into three groups: (A) could hire me again or need my services now, (B) probably won't hire me but are well placed to refer clients like [describe best client], and (C) worth staying in touch with but not a priority. For each person in A and B, give me one sentence on why, and the most natural reason to reach out right now. List: [paste your list]

Most people find 5 to 10 names in group A that they had completely forgotten about. Start there.

Step 2: Reach out with a reason, not a request

The message that doesn't work is “Hey! Long time no talk. Let me know if you need anything!” It's vague, it's about you, and it gives them nothing to reply to.

The message that works has three parts: a specific memory or observation about them, one short update about what you're doing now, and an easy question. AI can draft it in seconds once it knows how you write.

2

Draft a reconnect message in your voice

Prompt

Here are three messages I've written that sound like me: [paste 3 short emails or DMs]. Match that tone. Write a short message (under 80 words) to [name], who I know from [context]. Open with something specific: [a project we did, a post of theirs I liked, a change at their company]. Then share one sentence on what I'm doing now: [your current offer in plain words]. End with one easy question about them, not a pitch. No exclamation marks, no “I hope this finds you well.” Give me two versions, one for email and one for a LinkedIn message.

Here's what a good result looks like:

Hi Priya, I saw your team just launched the new onboarding program. Congrats, that was a big lift when we talked about it last spring.

I've been spending most of my time setting up AI-assisted support inboxes for small agencies, which has been fun.

How's the new program landing with clients so far?

No ask. That's on purpose. The first message restarts the conversation. Most replies will naturally include “what are you up to these days?” or a problem they're dealing with, and that's your opening.

Always edit before you send. AI gets you 80% of the way there. Change one detail, cut a sentence, make it sound like you said it out loud. People can tell when a message was pasted.

Step 3: Make the right ask for the right person

Once someone replies and the conversation is warm, there are only three asks worth making. Match the ask to the group from Step 1.

  1. Work again (group A). “Based on what you said about [problem], I could help with that. Want to grab 20 minutes next week to see if it's a fit?”
  2. A specific referral (group B). Not “know anyone who needs help?” but “Do you know any [specific type of business] owners who are [specific situation]? I'm looking to help two more this quarter.”
  3. A partnership (other service providers). “We seem to serve the same clients. Want to swap notes on how we each work, so we know when to send people each other's way?”

Specific asks get specific answers. A vague ask makes people search their whole memory, come up empty, and say “I'll keep you in mind,” which almost never turns into anything.

3

Turn a reply into the right next step

Prompt

I reconnected with [name], who I know from [context]. Here's our conversation so far: [paste]. Based on what they've said, should I (a) offer to help them directly, (b) ask for a specific referral, or (c) suggest a referral partnership? Explain why in one sentence, then draft my reply in my voice, under 70 words, with one clear and easy next step.

Step 4: Make referring you effortless

The biggest reason people don't refer you isn't that they don't want to. It's that they don't know how to describe what you do. Fix that by handing them the words.

Write a short “forwardable” blurb they can paste into an email or a Slack message: who you help, the result you get, and how to reach you. When someone says “I might know someone,” reply with “Thank you! Here's a short note you can forward if it's easier.”

4

Write your forwardable referral blurb

Prompt

Write a 3-sentence blurb that someone could forward to a friend to introduce me. I'm [name], a [role] who helps [who] get [result]. A good fit is someone who [specific situation or trigger]. One recent result: [short client result, no names]. Write it in the third person, warm and plain, and end with how to reach me: [link or email]. Then write a one-line version for a text message.

Step 5: Follow up once, then stay in touch

Some people won't reply. That's normal, and it's rarely personal. Inboxes are busy. Send one short follow-up about a week later that gives them something useful, like a relevant article or a quick idea for their business. If there's still nothing, move them to a light-touch list and check in again in two or three months.

This is where an AI assistant really earns its keep. In ChatGPT or Claude you can set up a recurring task that runs on the first Monday of every month, looks at your tracker, and gives you five people to reconnect with and a reason to contact each one. It takes the remembering off your plate entirely. I walk through how to build that kind of scheduled agent, step by step, in 20 AI Agents That Pay for Themselves.

Step 6: Keep it to 15 minutes a week

The whole system falls apart if it's a one-time burst. You'll get a couple of replies, land something, get busy, and forget about it until the work dries up again. Instead, block 15 minutes on the same day every week:

  • Send 3 to 5 reconnect messages from your Warm 50
  • Reply to anyone who wrote back, and make the right ask
  • Send any follow-ups that are due
  • Add one new name to the list (someone you met or worked with this week)

That's 150 to 250 warm conversations a year from 15 minutes a week. Even if only a small share turn into work or referrals, that's a pipeline most cold campaigns can't match.

Three mistakes to avoid

  • The mass blast. Sending the same “I'm taking on new clients!” message to everyone. It reads as desperate and gets ignored. One person, one specific message.
  • Letting AI write it alone. Unedited AI messages are easy to spot and they quietly spend the trust you've built. Always add a real detail only you would know.
  • Pitching in the first message. Restart the relationship first. The ask comes after they've replied.

Your next step

Block an hour this week. Build your Warm 50, run the sorting prompt, and send your first five messages. You'll likely have a reply before the end of the day.
