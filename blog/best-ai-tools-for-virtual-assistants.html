What are the best AI tools for virtual assistants?

There are thousands of AI tools and new ones every week. A virtual assistant needs about four. Here's the short list, organized by the job each one does, plus the test to run before any of them touches a client's data.

By Denise Cullom · November 5, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

The best AI tool stack for most virtual assistants is small: one general AI assistant (ChatGPT or Claude, about $20 a month for the paid plan), a meeting recorder that produces transcripts (such as Fathom or Otter), one automation tool to move information between apps (such as Zapier), and the AI already built into your clients' apps (Gmail, Microsoft 365, Notion, Canva). Learn one assistant deeply rather than five shallowly. Skip single-purpose AI writing tools, which mostly repackage the same models at a higher price. Before any tool touches client information, check five things: who owns the account, whether data is used for training, where it's stored, who else can see it, and how to delete it.

The tool lists you'll find online tend to have 50 entries, half of them affiliate links to tools that will be gone next year. That's not how working VAs actually operate. The ones making good money from AI work run a short, boring stack and know it inside out.

This builds on how to become an AI virtual assistant. If you haven't picked a first task to hand to AI yet, start there.

Which AI tools does a virtual assistant actually need?

Think in jobs, not brands. A VA's AI work needs four things:

  1. A brain: one AI assistant that drafts, summarizes, sorts, researches and follows your saved instructions.
  2. Ears: something that turns calls and meetings into a transcript the brain can work from.
  3. Hands: an automation tool that moves things between apps on a trigger, like a new form entry or a new receipt.
  4. The client's own tools: the AI features already inside the software the client pays for.

That's it. Everything else is optional until a specific client workflow needs it.

Which AI assistant should a VA use?

ChatGPT or Claude. Both are excellent at the core VA jobs: drafting emails, summarizing transcripts, reformatting documents, writing procedures and following a long set of standing instructions. Both let you save instructions and files in a project so you're not re-explaining a client every time. Both have a paid individual plan at about $20 a month, which is worth it the moment you use AI for paid work.

Pick one and get good at it. The VA who knows one assistant well, with a library of tested instructions for each client, will outperform the one who hops between five every week. There's a full side-by-side coming up in this series on choosing between ChatGPT and Claude.

What about meeting notes and transcripts?

Meeting follow-up is one of the easiest AI services to sell, and it needs a transcript. A recorder like Fathom or Otter joins the call (or records on a phone for in-person meetings) and gives you the text. Your AI assistant then turns it into a summary, action items with owners and a draft follow-up email in the client's voice.

Many video platforms now produce their own transcripts too. If the client already has one, use it before adding another tool.

Do you need an automation tool like Zapier?

Not on day one. You need one when a workflow has a trigger: “every time a form is filled in,” “every time a receipt lands in this inbox,” “every Monday at 8am.” Zapier is the most widely used and the easiest to learn; Make is a popular alternative. Both have free tiers that are enough to learn on.

Before you build an automation, check whether the AI assistant can now do the job itself. Both major assistants can connect to some common apps and run tasks on a schedule, which covers a lot of simple workflows without a separate tool.

What about the AI inside the client's apps?

This is the most overlooked part of the stack and often the safest place to start. If a client lives in Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, their AI features work inside the account the client already controls, under the agreements the client already has. Notion, Canva, HubSpot, QuickBooks and most help desk tools now have AI built in as well.

The advantage for you: no new account, no new data agreement, and when you hand the work over it stays with the client.

What does a VA's AI stack cost?

  • AI assistant: about $20 a month for a paid individual plan. Business or team plans cost more and add admin controls.
  • Meeting recorder: free plans are often enough to start; paid plans are typically in the $10 to $30 a month range.
  • Automation: free to learn on; paid plans start at around $20 to $30 a month once workflows run often.
  • Client app AI: usually paid for by the client as part of their plan.

Prices change often, so check the tool's own pricing page. But the shape holds: your own stack costs under $75 a month, and a single AI service sold to a single client covers it several times over. Where a tool is used only for one client, it's reasonable for the client to pay for it directly.

Which AI tools should a VA skip?

  • Single-purpose AI writers. Most are a general AI model with a template on top and a higher price. Your assistant plus a good saved instruction does the same job.
  • Anything that wants your client's email password. Proper tools connect through a secure sign-in the client approves, not a password you type in.
  • Tools with no clear data policy. If you can't find out where data goes and how to delete it in five minutes, it doesn't go near client work.
  • The tool of the week. If it's three weeks old and has a waitlist, let someone else test it on their clients first.

How do you check a tool is safe for client data?

Five questions, every time, before a tool sees anything from a client:

  1. Who owns the account? Ideally the client, so the data and history stay with their business.
  2. Is the data used for training? Find the setting and turn it off, or use a business plan where it's off by default.
  3. Where is it stored, and for how long?
  4. Who else can see it? Team members, other workspaces, the tool's staff.
  5. How do you delete it when the work ends?

How can AI help you choose tools?

1

Build the tool stack for one client

Prompt

Act as a practical AI consultant for virtual assistants. I support a [type of business] client. They already use: [list their apps, for example Gmail, Google Drive, Calendly, QuickBooks, Slack]. I use [ChatGPT or Claude] on a paid plan. The workflow I want to set up is: [describe it]. Step 1: Tell me whether this can be done with the AI assistant and the client's existing apps alone, and how. Step 2: Only if it can't, name the one extra tool type I'd need and why. Step 3: List the exact data the workflow will touch and flag anything sensitive. Step 4: Give me the five questions I should answer about each tool's data handling before I start, specific to this workflow. Keep it as simple as possible.

2

Check a new AI tool before you use it

Prompt

I'm a virtual assistant considering [tool name] for client work, specifically to [job]. Here is the text of its privacy policy and pricing page: [paste]. Step 1: In plain English, explain what happens to the data I put in, whether it's used for training, how long it's kept and how to delete it. Step 2: List anything a careful small-business client would be uncomfortable with. Step 3: Tell me whether the same job could be done with a general AI assistant I already pay for. Step 4: Give a clear recommendation: use it, use it only with non-sensitive data, or skip it. Only use what's in the text I pasted; say so if something isn't covered.

The tool is never the product. The product is the hours you save the client. A short stack you know well gets you there faster than a long one you're always learning.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best AI tool for a virtual assistant?

A general AI assistant, either ChatGPT or Claude on a paid plan. It covers drafting, summarizing, research, reformatting and following saved instructions for each client, which is most of what a VA needs AI for.

How many AI tools does a VA need?

Usually four: one AI assistant, a meeting recorder for transcripts, one automation tool such as Zapier when a workflow needs a trigger, and the AI features already built into the client's own apps.

How much do AI tools cost for a virtual assistant?

A typical VA stack costs under $75 a month: about $20 for an AI assistant, a free or low-cost meeting recorder, and a free or starter automation plan. Tools used for one client can be billed to that client.

Should virtual assistants use ChatGPT or Claude?

Either works well for VA tasks, and both cost about $20 a month for a paid individual plan. The bigger win is choosing one and building a library of tested instructions for each client rather than switching between tools.

Is Zapier necessary for AI virtual assistants?

Not at first. You need an automation tool when a workflow has a trigger, such as a new form entry or a new receipt. Check first whether the AI assistant or the client's own apps can do the job without it.

How do I know if an AI tool is safe for client data?

Answer five questions: who owns the account, whether data is used for training, where it's stored and for how long, who else can see it, and how to delete it. If you can't find the answers quickly, don't use it with client information.

Which AI tools should VAs avoid?

Single-purpose AI writers that repackage the same models at a higher price, anything that asks for a client's password instead of a secure sign-in, tools with no clear data policy, and brand-new tools nobody has tested on real client work yet.
