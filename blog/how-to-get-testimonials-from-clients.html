How do you get testimonials and case studies from clients that win you more work?

A specific testimonial from a happy client does more selling than anything you can say about yourself. Most freelancers just never ask, or ask in a way that gets back “She was great to work with!” Here's how to get the kind that wins work.

By Denise Cullom · October 23, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer

Ask right after a visible win, while the client is happiest, not months later. Make it easy: send three short questions (what was going on before, what changed, who they'd recommend you to) or ask for ten minutes on a call and offer to write it up for their approval. The best testimonials name a specific problem and a specific result, ideally with a number. Turn your strongest results into short before-and-after case studies, and put them where buyers decide: your website, your LinkedIn profile, your proposals and your follow-up messages. AI can turn a rambling answer or call transcript into a clean draft, but only publish words the client has approved.

When I was buying services as a COO, I always read the testimonials, but I skipped the vague ones. “A pleasure to work with” told me nothing. What I looked for was someone like me describing a problem like mine and what changed. One of those was worth more than a page of the vendor's own claims.

Your clients' buyers think the same way. The good news is that most happy clients are glad to help. They're just busy, and they don't know what to write. Your job is to make it easy and to ask the right questions.

Why do testimonials matter so much for freelancers?

Because buying a service is a leap of faith. A client can't try your work before they pay, so they look for evidence that someone like them took the same leap and it worked out. A specific testimonial does three things: it shows you've solved this problem before, it shows a real person was happy enough to say so, and it gives the buyer words to explain the decision to their partner or boss.

It also does something for you. Reading back what clients say you changed for them makes it a lot easier to state your price with confidence.

When is the best time to ask for a testimonial?

Right after a moment the client can feel. That might be:

  • The day they hit the result you promised, or a milestone along the way.
  • When they thank you or say something kind in an email or on a call.
  • When they renew, extend or hire you for a second project.
  • When they refer someone to you.
  • At the end of a project, as part of your wrap-up.

The worst time is months after the work ended, when the details have faded. If a client sends you a glowing message, reply with thanks and ask, the same day, whether you could share it.

What questions get the best testimonials?

Never ask “could you write me a testimonial?” It's a blank page, and blank pages get vague answers. Ask three questions instead:

  1. “What was going on before we worked together?” This gets the problem, in their words, which is exactly what future buyers need to recognize themselves.
  2. “What changed?” Follow up with “can you put a number on it?” Hours saved, revenue added, invoices paid on time, clients signed.
  3. “Who would you recommend me to?” This often produces the most quotable line, and sometimes an actual referral.

If you want one more, ask “what almost stopped you from hiring me?” The answer tells you which doubt to address on your sales page, and a testimonial that says “I wasn't sure it was worth the money, but…” is very persuasive.

How do you make it easy for busy clients?

Give them three ways to say yes, and let them pick:

  • Reply to three questions by email. Short answers are fine. You'll shape them.
  • A ten-minute call. Ask the questions, record with permission, and write it up from the conversation. People say much more when they're talking than when they're typing.
  • You draft, they approve. Write a short draft from what they've already told you, and ask them to edit or approve it. Many busy clients prefer this.

Whichever way you go, always get approval for the final wording and ask how they'd like to be named: full name and company, first name and industry, or anonymous. Ask whether you can use a photo or their logo. And never write words they didn't say or approve.

What makes a testimonial believable?

Compare these two:

“Jess is amazing! Highly recommend!”

“I was answering client emails until 11pm and our invoices went out two weeks late. Within a month of working with Jess, my inbox was under control and we were getting paid on time. I got my evenings back. If you run a small agency and admin is eating your week, talk to her.” Owner, five-person design agency

The second one works because it has a specific before, a specific after, a timeframe and who it's for. It's also written the way people actually talk. Don't polish testimonials until they sound like marketing copy, because buyers can tell.

What is a case study, and do you need one?

A case study is a testimonial with the story filled in. You don't need a glossy ten-page document. A one-page case study with five parts is plenty:

  1. The client: who they are, in a sentence (with permission, or described anonymously).
  2. The problem: what was going wrong and what it was costing them.
  3. What you did: your approach in three or four steps.
  4. The result: what changed, with numbers and a timeframe.
  5. In their words: the testimonial quote.

Two or three short case studies covering your main client types is enough. When a prospect says “have you worked with someone like me?”, you send the one that fits.

What if you're new and don't have clients yet?

You still have options. Ask former employers, colleagues or managers for a recommendation about your skills and reliability. Do one or two small projects at a reduced rate for people who fit your ideal client, in exchange for honest feedback and a testimonial if they're happy. Or help someone in your network with a specific, short piece of work. What you shouldn't do is invent testimonials, use stock photos of “clients,” or pay for reviews. It's dishonest, and in many places it's illegal.

Where should you use testimonials and case studies?

Put them where buyers are making up their minds, not on a page nobody visits:

  • Your homepage and service pages, right next to the price or the booking button.
  • Your LinkedIn profile: ask happy clients for a LinkedIn recommendation, and mention results in your About section and Featured links.
  • Your proposals: one short quote from a similar client, near the investment section.
  • Your follow-up messages: a short client story is one of the best ways to follow up without just “checking in.”
  • Your posts: a before-and-after story, with permission, makes a strong post.

How can AI help you collect and use testimonials?

AI is good at the parts that slow people down: writing the ask, shaping a rambling answer into something readable, and turning a result into a case study. It must never make up details. Start with the request:

1

Write the testimonial request

Prompt

My voice rules: [paste]. Write a short, warm message to [client name], who I've worked with on [project] for [time]. We just [milestone or result]. Thank them for something specific, then ask if they'd be willing to share a few words about working together. Give them three options: answer three short questions by email (what was going on before we worked together, what changed and can they put a number on it, and who they'd recommend me to), a ten-minute call, or I draft something from what they've already said for them to approve. Under 120 words. Make it easy to say no.

When the answers come back, or you have a call transcript, shape them without changing the meaning:

2

Shape their words into a testimonial

Prompt

Here is what my client [name] said about working with me: [paste their answers or the call transcript]. Write three versions of a testimonial: one under 40 words, one under 80 words and one under 150 words. Use only their words and meaning, lightly edited for clarity. Keep their tone and any numbers exactly as they said them. Each version should cover what was going on before, what changed, and who they'd recommend me to. Don't add any claims, numbers or praise they didn't give. Then list any edits you made so I can show them to the client for approval.

And for your strongest results, build a one-page case study:

3

Turn a result into a case study

Prompt

Write a one-page case study (under 350 words) from these notes about a client project: [paste notes, results and the approved testimonial]. Use five sections: the client in one sentence ([how they want to be named]), the problem and what it was costing them, what I did in three or four steps, the result with numbers and a timeframe, and the testimonial quote. Plain language, written for a future client who has the same problem. Give it a headline that states the result. Only use facts from my notes, and flag anything I should confirm with the client before publishing.

Before you paste client details into any AI tool, leave out anything confidential and anything the client hasn't agreed to share. Send the final version to the client for approval before it goes anywhere public.

Frequently asked questions

How do you ask a client for a testimonial?

Ask right after a result they can feel, and make it easy. Instead of asking them to write a testimonial from scratch, send three short questions: what was going on before you worked together, what changed, and who they would recommend you to. Or offer a ten-minute call, or to draft something for their approval.

What questions should you ask for a testimonial?

Ask what was going on before you worked together, what changed (and whether they can put a number on it), and who they would recommend you to. Optionally ask what almost stopped them from hiring you, which helps you answer doubts future buyers have.

Can I write a testimonial for my client to approve?

Yes, as long as it is based on what they actually told you and they approve the final wording and how they are named. Never publish words a client hasn't said or approved.

What makes a good testimonial?

A specific problem, a specific result with a number or timeframe, who it is for, and natural language that sounds like a real person. Vague praise like “great to work with” is far less persuasive.

How do I get testimonials when I'm just starting out?

Ask former colleagues or managers for recommendations about your skills, or do one or two small projects for ideal clients in exchange for honest feedback and a testimonial if they are happy. Never invent testimonials or pay for reviews.

Where should I put testimonials?

Where buyers decide: next to prices and booking buttons on your website, in your LinkedIn profile and recommendations, in proposals near the investment section, and in follow-up messages as short client stories.

Can AI write testimonials for me?

AI should only shape what a real client said, never invent it. Use it to draft your request, tidy up a client's answers or call transcript without changing their meaning, and turn a real result into a case study, then get the client's approval before publishing.
