How should freelancers price their services and write a proposal that gets signed?

Most freelancers set prices by guessing what others charge and then knocking a bit off. Here's a calmer way to price, a one-page proposal clients actually read, and the AI prompts that help you write it the same day as the call.

By Denise Cullom · October 21, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer

Price the result, not your hours. Start from what the problem costs the client each month and what solving it is worth, then set a price that's a small fraction of that value and still pays you well. Package the work as a fixed-price first step, and when it makes sense offer three options (starter, recommended, premium) so they choose how to work with you, not whether to. Send a one-page proposal within 24 hours of the call: their situation in their words, the result, your approach, timeline, price and one clear way to say yes. If budget is tight, cut scope, never your price.

When I was a COO, I signed a lot of proposals from vendors and consultants. The ones I said yes to fastest had two things in common: they were short, and they described my problem better than I could. The ones that stalled were long, full of the vendor's credentials, and priced by the hour, which left me doing math on how many hours they'd really need.

Pricing and proposals feel like the scary part of getting clients, but they're mostly about one shift: stop selling your time, and start selling a result someone has already told you they want.

Should you charge by the hour or by the project?

For most freelancers, VAs, consultants and coaches, fixed prices for a defined result work better than hourly rates, for both of you:

  • The client knows the cost up front. No surprise invoices, no watching the clock.
  • You get rewarded for getting faster. With hourly billing, every shortcut you learn, including every way AI speeds you up, cuts your income. With a fixed price, it raises your profit.
  • The conversation is about value. “Your invoices go out on time every week” is easier to say yes to than “ten hours of admin support.”

Hourly still makes sense for genuinely open-ended work, like ad-hoc support with no clear scope. Even then, a monthly retainer with a defined number of hours and a clear list of what's included is easier to sell and easier to manage.

How do you work out what to charge?

Use three numbers, and let the highest sensible one win:

  1. Your floor. What you need to earn per month, divided by the number of clients you can serve well. This is the price below which the work isn't worth taking. Most people discover their floor is higher than what they've been charging.
  2. The market. What others with similar results charge for similar work. It's a sanity check, not a target.
  3. The value. What the problem costs the client each month, or what the result is worth to them. If late invoicing costs a business $3,000 a month, a $1,200 fix is an easy decision. If you never ask what the problem costs, you'll never know this number, which is why it's the most important question on a discovery call.

A good rule of thumb is that your price should feel like a small fraction of the value, often a tenth to a third of what the result is worth over a year. That's what lets you state it calmly: you know it's a good deal for them.

How do you package your services?

Turn what you do into something with a name, a result, a timeline and a price. For example:

  • Instead of “social media help,” offer a “30-day content reset”: a content plan, 12 posts written and scheduled, and a template so the client can keep going.
  • Instead of “VA hours,” offer “Inbox and calendar handover”: two weeks to set up rules, templates and a weekly routine, then a monthly retainer to run it.
  • Instead of “business coaching,” offer “90-day pricing sprint”: new packages, new prices and the scripts to sell them, with six calls.

A small first step is the easiest thing to buy. Once the client sees results, the next, bigger piece of work almost sells itself.

Should you offer three pricing options?

Often, yes. Three options change the client's question from “should I hire this person?” to “which way do I want to work with them?” Keep them simple:

  • Starter: the core result with the essentials. Smallest price.
  • Recommended: the option you'd choose for them, with what makes the result stick. Mark it clearly as your recommendation.
  • Premium: everything in Recommended plus speed, extra support or a bigger scope. It makes the middle option look sensible, and some clients will take it.

Make each step up add something the client can see, not just “more hours.” If you only have one obvious offer, one recommended option is fine. Three options that are confusing are worse than one that's clear.

What should a proposal include?

A proposal isn't a brochure. It's a one-page summary of the conversation you already had, with a price and a next step. Six parts are enough:

  1. Their situation, in their own words from the call.
  2. The result they want, including the number they said would prove it worked.
  3. Your approach, in three steps they can picture.
  4. Timeline, with a start date.
  5. Investment, one recommended option or three options, next to the cost of the problem.
  6. Next step, exactly how to say yes: reply to this email, sign here, or pay this link.

Leave out your life story, a long list of services, and anything they didn't talk about. If they need proof, one short client result is plenty.

When should you send the proposal?

Within 24 hours of the call, while the conversation is fresh for both of you. Better still, walk them through it on a short call instead of only emailing a PDF, so you can answer questions on the spot. And never send a price they haven't heard before: talk about the investment on the discovery call so the proposal confirms what they already know. A surprise number in a PDF is the most common reason proposals go quiet.

Before you hit send, put a decision call or a follow-up date on the calendar. If they go quiet anyway, follow up on day 2, day 5 and day 10 with something useful, as in this follow-up guide.

What do you do when a client says it's too expensive?

Don't drop the price for the same work. That teaches the client your prices are made up. Instead:

  1. Ask what they're comparing it to. Sometimes it's a cheaper freelancer offering something different. Sometimes it's nothing, and they just need to hear the value again.
  2. Remind them of their number. “You mentioned this is costing you about $3,000 a month. This pays for itself in under two weeks.”
  3. Offer a smaller scope. “If budget is the main concern, we could start with just the first part for $600 and add the rest once you see it working.”

If it's still a no, let them go kindly. Not every client is the right client, and a client who resents your price will rarely be happy with your work.

How do you raise your rates?

Raise prices for new clients first. It's low risk: nobody knows your old price, and your proposals and three options do the work. For existing clients, give 30 to 60 days' notice in a short, friendly message that thanks them, states the new rate and the date it starts, and reminds them of a result you've delivered. Most will stay. If you're fully booked, your waitlist is growing, or nobody ever pushes back on price, those are all signs you're charging too little.

How can AI help with pricing and proposals?

AI can't tell you what you're worth, but it's very good at the math, the packaging and the first draft. Start by building your options:

1

Package and price your offer

Prompt

I'm a [your role] who helps [type of client] with [result]. I currently charge [current price or hourly rate] and I want to earn [monthly income goal] working with about [number] clients at a time. A typical client's problem costs them about [monthly cost, if you know it]. Step 1: Work out my price floor per client. Step 2: Suggest a named, fixed-price first-step offer with a clear result and timeline. Step 3: Design three options (starter, recommended, premium) where each step up adds something the client can see, and suggest a price for each with your reasoning. Step 4: Write one sentence I can say on a call that compares the recommended price to the cost of the problem. Ask me questions first if anything important is missing.

Then turn your call notes into a proposal on the same day:

2

Draft a one-page proposal

Prompt

My voice rules: [paste]. Here are my notes from a discovery call with [name] at [company]: [paste notes]. Write a one-page proposal under 400 words with these sections: their situation in their own words (quote my notes where you can), the result they want and the number they'll measure it by, my approach in three steps, timeline with a start date of [date], investment of [price or three options, with the recommended one marked], and the next step, which is [how to say yes]. Plain language, no jargon, no section about me beyond one sentence. Then list anything in my notes that's unclear and that I should confirm before sending.

Finally, rehearse the hard part, which is saying the number and handling the pushback:

3

Prepare for price objections

Prompt

I'm proposing [offer] at [price] to [type of client], whose problem costs them about [monthly cost]. List the five price objections they're most likely to raise. For each, write a calm, honest two-sentence response in my voice ([paste voice rules]) that never lowers the price for the same work, and where it fits, offers a smaller scope instead. Then play the client and push back on my price so I can practice, one message at a time, and give me feedback after five exchanges.

Check every number AI suggests against your own floor and what you know about the client. It's a thinking partner, not a pricing authority.

Frequently asked questions

Should freelancers charge hourly or per project?

For most freelancers, a fixed price for a defined result works better. The client knows the cost up front, the conversation is about value instead of time, and you earn more as you get faster. Hourly or a monthly retainer with set hours still suits open-ended support work.

How do I figure out what to charge as a freelancer?

Look at three numbers: your floor (the income you need divided by how many clients you can serve well), what the market charges for similar results, and what the problem costs the client. Price at a small fraction of the value to the client, as long as it is above your floor.

Should I give clients three pricing options?

Often, yes. A starter, a recommended and a premium option let the client choose how to work with you instead of whether to. Mark the recommended one clearly and make each step up add something the client can see. If you only have one clear offer, one option is fine.

What should be in a freelance proposal?

Keep it to one page: the client's situation in their own words, the result they want, your approach in three steps, the timeline, the price, and exactly how to say yes. Leave out long credentials and anything you didn't discuss on the call.

How soon should you send a proposal after a call?

Within 24 hours, and make sure the price isn't a surprise because you already discussed it on the call. Book a short decision call or a follow-up date before you send it.

What do you say when a client says your price is too high?

Ask what they are comparing it to, remind them what the problem is costing them, and if budget is genuinely tight, offer a smaller scope rather than a lower price for the same work.

Can AI help me write proposals?

Yes. Paste your call notes and AI can draft a one-page proposal in your voice, suggest how to package your options and help you rehearse price objections. Check every number and promise yourself before you send it.
