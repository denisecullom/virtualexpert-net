How do you choose AI tools that are safe and worth paying for in a small business?

There's a new AI tool for everything, and most of them promise to save you hours. Some will. Some will quietly put your customer data somewhere you didn't expect. Here's how a small business can tell the difference before signing up.

By Denise Cullom · October 24, 2026 · 9 min read

The short answer

Start with one costly workflow, not a tool. Write down the job, how long it takes and what it costs, then shortlist two or three tools that do that job. For each one, check what happens to your data (is it used for training, where is it stored, can you delete it), who can see it (admin controls, single sign-on, permission settings), how it connects to the systems you already use, and what it really costs per person. Prefer business plans over personal ones for company data. Then run a two-week trial on real work with a number to beat, and only roll it out if it beats it. Write a one-page rule for what staff may and may not paste in.

I've spent a lot of my career choosing software for growing companies, and the expensive mistakes almost always started the same way: someone saw a great demo, bought it, and then went looking for a problem it could solve. AI tools make that mistake easier than ever, because the demos are genuinely impressive and the free trial is one click away.

The fix isn't to avoid AI. It's to pick tools the way a careful operator picks any vendor: start from the work, check the risks, test it on real tasks and measure the result.

Why does it matter which AI tool you choose?

Because whatever your team types or uploads goes somewhere. Customer emails, contracts, financial reports and employee details are exactly what people paste into AI tools to save time. Depending on the tool and the plan, that information may be stored for a long time, reviewed by the vendor, used to improve their models, or shared with other services the tool connects to. Most small businesses never check. It also matters for cost: a dozen overlapping subscriptions add up fast, and a tool nobody uses after the first week is pure waste.

Where should you start?

With one workflow that costs you real time or money. Write down:

  • The job: for example, “answering customer questions about orders” or “writing meeting notes and follow-up tasks.”
  • Who does it and how long it takes each week.
  • What it costs: hours times hourly cost, plus any mistakes or delays it causes.
  • What data it touches: customer details, money, health information, employee records.
  • What good looks like: the number a tool would need to beat to be worth it.

That one page turns “which AI tool should we buy?” into a question you can actually answer. If you're not sure which workflow to start with, this guide to the first tasks to hand to AI will help.

Should you use a general AI assistant or a specialist tool?

Often, a general assistant on a business plan, like Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini, can handle the workflow with good instructions and connections to the tools you already use. That means one vendor to check, one bill and one set of rules for your team. A specialist tool makes sense when it does something the general assistant can't do well, such as handling phone calls, working inside a specific industry system, or meeting a compliance requirement your business has. Before you buy a specialist tool, ask whether it's really just a general model with a nicer screen and a higher price.

What questions should you ask before using an AI tool?

Here's a 12-question checklist. You'll find most answers on the vendor's pricing page, privacy policy, security or trust page and help center. If you can't find an answer, email them and ask. How quickly and clearly they reply tells you a lot.

Your data

  1. Is our data used to train the vendor's models? On which plans, and is it on or off by default?
  2. How long is our data kept, and can we delete it? Including chat history, uploaded files and anything connected.
  3. Where is the data stored and processed? This matters if you have customers in places with strict privacy laws.
  4. Who at the vendor can see our data, and when? Look for a clear answer, not “we take privacy seriously.”

Security and control

  1. Can we manage users centrally? Admin controls, removing someone when they leave, and ideally single sign-on.
  2. What can it access, and can we limit it? If it connects to your email, files or CRM, can you choose exactly what it reads and whether it can send, change or delete anything?
  3. Does it have independent security checks? A SOC 2 report or ISO 27001 certification is a good sign for a business tool, and the vendor should share details on request.
  4. Will they sign the agreements we need? A data processing agreement if you handle personal data, or a business associate agreement if you handle health information in the US.

Fit and value

  1. Does it connect to the tools we already use? A tool that needs everything copied in by hand rarely sticks.
  2. Can we try it on real work before paying? Without putting sensitive data in during the trial.
  3. What does it really cost? Per person, per month, including usage limits, add-ons and the plan you'd actually need for the controls above.
  4. What happens if we leave? Can you export your data, instructions and history in a usable format?

What are the red flags?

  • No clear privacy policy or security page, or one that doesn't mention AI or customer data at all.
  • Your data trains their models and you can't turn it off on a paid business plan.
  • It asks for full access to everything (all of your email, all of your files) with no way to narrow it.
  • No company behind it that you can identify, or no way to reach a person.
  • Promises that sound too good: “replace your whole team,” “100% accurate,” “fully automated sales.”
  • Only personal or free plans for something your whole team will use with company data.
  • Browser extensions from unknown developers that can read every page you visit.

How do you test an AI tool before you buy it?

Run a two-week trial with a clear goal. A good trial looks like this:

  1. Pick one workflow and one or two people who do that work every week.
  2. Measure the “before”: how long the task takes now and how often it has errors.
  3. Use real but non-sensitive work, or remove names, numbers and personal details from real examples.
  4. Write down the “after”: time per task, quality, mistakes caught, and how much checking it needed.
  5. Decide against your number. If it beat it, roll it out with simple rules. If it didn't, cancel and try the next tool on your shortlist.

Ask the people doing the trial what they thought, too. A tool that saves time on paper but that your team finds frustrating won't get used.

What rules should your team follow when using AI tools?

Even a five-person business needs a one-page rule. Keep it short enough that people actually read it:

  • Which tools are approved, and that company work goes only into those, on company accounts.
  • What never goes in: passwords, payment card numbers, health information, other people's personal details unless the tool is approved for it, and anything under a confidentiality agreement.
  • A person checks before it goes out: anything AI writes for a customer, or anything involving money, contracts or legal claims, gets reviewed by a human.
  • Who to ask before trying a new tool, and how to report a mistake.

Review the list of approved tools every few months. AI vendors change their plans and policies often, so check the privacy and pricing pages again when you renew.

How can AI help you choose AI tools?

Use a general assistant you already trust to do the reading and organizing, then verify anything important on the vendor's own pages. First, define the job:

1

Define the workflow and the number to beat

Prompt

I run a [type of business] with [number] people. I'm considering an AI tool for this workflow: [describe the task, who does it and how often]. It currently takes about [hours] a week, and [describe any errors or delays]. Step 1: Write a one-page brief for this workflow covering the job, the people involved, the data it touches (flag anything sensitive), the current cost per month at [hourly cost], and a clear success measure for a two-week trial. Step 2: List what a tool would need to do well for this workflow, and what it must never do. Step 3: Tell me whether this sounds like a job for a general AI assistant with good instructions or a specialist tool, and why.

Then compare your shortlist using the vendors' own documents:

2

Compare tools against the checklist

Prompt

I'm comparing these AI tools for [workflow]: [tool names and the plans I'm considering]. Here is text from each vendor's pricing page, privacy policy and security page: [paste]. Using only the text I pasted, fill in a table with one row per tool and these columns: is our data used for training and can we turn it off, data retention and deletion, where data is stored, admin controls and single sign-on, what it can access and whether access can be limited, security certifications mentioned, agreements offered (such as a data processing agreement), integrations with [our tools], real monthly cost for [number] users, and how to export our data. Write "not stated" wherever the text doesn't answer the question. Then list the questions I should email each vendor, and any red flags.

And once you've picked, write the rules your team will follow:

3

Write a one-page AI use rule

Prompt

Write a one-page AI use rule for my [type of business] with [number] people. Approved tools: [list, with plan names]. We handle [types of data, such as customer contact details, payment information or health information]. Include: which tools are approved and that company work stays on company accounts, a short list of what must never be pasted into any AI tool, which outputs a person must check before they go to a customer, who to ask before trying a new tool, and how to report a mistake. Plain language, under 400 words, friendly but clear, written for staff who aren't technical. Add a note that I should have it checked against any legal or industry rules that apply to us.

A checklist is a starting point, not legal advice. If you work in a regulated industry like healthcare, finance or law, or you handle a lot of personal data, have your choice and your rules reviewed by someone who knows those requirements.

Frequently asked questions

How do I choose the right AI tool for my small business?

Start from one workflow that costs you real time or money, not from a tool. Write down the job, who does it, how long it takes and what data it touches, shortlist two or three tools that do that job, check them for data handling, security, integrations and real cost, and run a two-week trial on real work with a number to beat.

Is it safe to put company data into AI tools?

It depends on the tool and the plan. Check whether your data is used for training and whether you can turn that off, how long it is kept, who at the vendor can see it, and what admin controls exist. Business plans usually give you more control than personal or free plans. Never paste passwords, payment card numbers or health information unless the tool is approved for it.

What questions should I ask an AI vendor?

Ask whether your data trains their models, how long it is kept and whether you can delete it, where it is stored, who can see it, what admin and access controls exist, which security certifications they hold, whether they will sign a data processing agreement, which of your tools it connects to, the real cost per person, and how you can export your data if you leave.

What are the red flags when choosing an AI tool?

No clear privacy or security information, training on your data with no way to opt out on a paid plan, demands for full access to all your email or files, no identifiable company or support contact, promises like “100% accurate” or “replace your whole team,” and browser extensions from unknown developers.

Should a small business use ChatGPT, Claude or a specialist AI tool?

Often a general assistant on a business plan can handle most workflows with good instructions and connections to your existing tools, which means fewer vendors and one set of rules. Choose a specialist tool when it does something the general assistant can't, such as handling phone calls or working inside an industry system you depend on.

Does a small business need an AI policy?

Yes, even a short one. A one-page rule should list approved tools, what must never be pasted in, which AI outputs a person must check before they reach a customer, and who to ask before trying a new tool.
