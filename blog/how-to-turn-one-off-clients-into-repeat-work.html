How do you turn one-off clients into repeat work and retainers?

Finding a new client costs far more than keeping a good one. Here's how to run a project so the client wants a second one, how to offer a retainer without it feeling like a sales pitch, and what to say to the ones who drifted away.

By Denise Cullom · October 28, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

Repeat work is won during the project, not after it. Start with a kickoff that sets expectations, send a short weekly update the client can forward to their boss or partner, and make the result visible with their own numbers. Then propose the next step before the work ends, while you're still in their calendar and their budget. When the work is ongoing by nature, offer a retainer with a clear monthly scope, a review point and a simple way to pause. And keep a list of past clients you check in with a few times a year: a short, generous message brings more work back than any cold campaign.

Most freelancers run their business like a leaky bucket. They pour effort into finding new clients, deliver well, then wave goodbye and start the hunt again. Meanwhile the easiest work they'll ever win is sitting in their sent folder: people who already trust them, already know how they work, and already have a budget line with their name on it.

In the companies I ran, keeping a customer cost a fraction of winning one, and the same is true for a one-person business. Here's how to stop the leak.

Why do good clients not come back?

Rarely because the work was bad. Usually because:

  • Nobody asked. The project ended, and no next step was ever proposed.
  • They forgot what you did. If the result was never shown in their own numbers, it fades.
  • They don't know what else you do. You're the invoicing person, so they hire someone else for the thing you'd have been perfect for.
  • The handover was a cliff. You finished, sent a final email, and disappeared.

All four are fixable, and three of them are fixed before the project is over.

How does good onboarding lead to repeat work?

The first week sets the tone for everything after it. A simple onboarding does four things:

  1. A welcome message that says what happens next, what you need from them and when the first thing lands.
  2. A kickoff call that confirms the result you're aiming at and the number you'll measure.
  3. Agreed communication: where you talk, how fast you reply, and when they'll hear from you each week.
  4. An early win in the first week or two, however small. Trust compounds from there.

Clients rarely remember an average project, but they remember feeling organized and looked after.

What should a weekly update say?

Three lines, every week, same day:

  • Done: what moved this week.
  • Next: what you're doing next week.
  • From you: anything you need from them, with a date.

Write it so the client can forward it without editing. It quietly reminds a busy person what they're paying for, it creates a written record when someone asks what you've been doing, and it gives their boss or business partner something to read, which matters when the renewal decision isn't theirs alone.

When should you propose the next piece of work?

Two or three weeks before the end, not on the last day. At that point the relationship is warm, the results are visible, and the client hasn't yet mentally closed the budget. A natural way to do it:

“We're on track to finish on the 14th. Looking at what we've uncovered, the two things that would make the biggest difference next are [X] and [Y]. Want me to put together what that would look like, or would you rather let this settle for a month first?”

You've given them a real choice, including the option to wait, which is what keeps it from feeling like a pitch. Either way you end the project with the end-of-project summary and a clear door left open.

When does a retainer make sense?

A retainer works when the need is ongoing rather than a one-time fix: an inbox that fills up every week, content that has to keep coming, books that close every month, a pipeline that needs working. It doesn't suit genuinely finite projects, and forcing one on a client who doesn't need it is how retainers get cancelled after two months.

Make it concrete. A retainer that just says “ten hours a month” invites the client to count hours; one built around what gets delivered invites them to count results:

  • What's included each month, described as outcomes with a fair-use limit if you need one.
  • What isn't, and how extra work gets priced.
  • The rhythm: your weekly update and a monthly call.
  • The review point: a check-in at three months to adjust.
  • How to pause or stop: usually 30 days' notice. Making it easy to leave makes it easier to start.

Price it on the value of the ongoing result, not on your hours, and review it once a year.

How do you win back a past client?

Gently, and with something useful in hand. Write to clients you'd happily work with again, say what made you think of them, share something specific you'd do differently now, and ask an open question. No guilt, no “just checking in,” no pretending you were about to call anyway.

Keep a simple schedule of check-ins through the year: a note after something good happens to them, a useful resource, a short message at a natural moment in their business. Three or four touches a year keeps you top of mind without becoming noise. That's the same give-first rhythm as in following up with prospects who stopped replying.

How do you ask for referrals from happy clients?

At a high point, not at the end of a long slog. When a client says something kind, that's the moment. Make it easy: name the type of person you're looking for, keep the ask small (“one or two people”), and offer a short blurb they can forward. Ask for a testimonial at the same time, using these three questions, because the answers often double as the introduction.

How can AI help you keep clients?

The follow-through is where most solo businesses lose repeat work, and that's exactly what AI is good at: turning scrappy notes into something the client values. Start with onboarding:

1

Build your onboarding pack

Prompt

My voice rules: [paste]. I'm a [your role] starting a new [offer] project with [client type]. The result we're aiming for is [result] and it takes [timeline]. Write me four things: a welcome email under 150 words that says what happens next and what I need from them, a 30-minute kickoff call agenda, a short list of what I need access to before day one, and the three expectations I should set out loud at kickoff to avoid the usual problems in this kind of project. Plain language, no jargon.

Then make the weekly update a two-minute job, from whatever notes you have:

2

Turn your notes into a weekly client update

Prompt

Here are my rough notes from this week on the [project] for [client]: [paste notes, task list or commit log]. Write a client update under 120 words with three headings: Done, Next, and What I need from you (with dates). Write it so the client could forward it to their business partner without editing. Factual and calm, no filler, no exclamation marks, and don't invent anything that isn't in my notes. Then list separately, just for me, anything in the notes I should raise as a risk before it becomes a problem.

And prepare the conversation about what comes next:

3

Plan the next step and the retainer offer

Prompt

I'm finishing a [offer] project for [client type]. Here's what we did and what changed, with numbers where I have them: [paste]. Here's what I noticed that still needs attention: [paste]. Step 1: Write a short end-of-project summary for the client, leading with their results in their own terms. Step 2: Suggest the two most valuable next pieces of work, with the result each one gets. Step 3: If ongoing support makes sense, draft a monthly retainer with what's included as outcomes, what's not, the rhythm of updates and calls, a three-month review and 30 days' notice to pause. Step 4: Write the message proposing it two weeks before the project ends, which gives them an easy way to say "not yet."

Keep client details out of any AI tool that isn't approved for them, and read every message before it goes out. These should sound like you on a good day, not like a report.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get repeat work from clients?

Win it during the project: set expectations at kickoff, send a short weekly update the client can forward, show results in their own numbers, and propose the next piece of work two or three weeks before the project ends rather than on the last day.

When should I offer a client a retainer?

When the need is ongoing rather than a one-time fix, such as an inbox, content, bookkeeping or a pipeline that needs steady attention. Offer it near the end of a successful project, and don't push one on a client whose need is genuinely finite.

What should a retainer include?

What's included each month described as outcomes, what isn't and how extra work is priced, the rhythm of updates and calls, a review point at about three months, and a simple way to pause with around 30 days' notice.

Should I charge a retainer by hours or by deliverables?

Deliverables and outcomes work better. Selling hours invites the client to count hours; selling a monthly result invites them to count the value. Add a fair-use limit if you need protection against scope creep.

How do I win back a past client?

Write a short, specific message: what made you think of them, something you would do differently or better now, and an open question. No guilt and no pressure. Keep a schedule of three or four genuine check-ins a year with clients you'd happily work with again.

How do I ask a client for a referral?

Ask at a high point, such as when they've just thanked you for a result. Name the kind of person you're looking for, keep the ask to one or two people, and offer a short blurb they can forward.

Can AI help with client updates and onboarding?

Yes. Paste your rough notes and AI can turn them into a weekly update the client can forward, draft welcome emails and kickoff agendas, and prepare an end-of-project summary and retainer proposal. Keep confidential details out of tools not approved for them, and read everything before sending.
