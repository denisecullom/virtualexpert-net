How do virtual assistants use AI to do research for clients?

"Can you look into this for me?" is one of the most common requests a VA gets, and one of the easiest to do badly. AI makes research much faster. It also makes it easy to hand a client something confident and wrong. Here's how to get the speed without the risk.

By Denise Cullom · November 13, 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer

Start by turning the request into one clear question with a deadline, a scope and a decision it supports. Use an AI tool with web search, and ask for sources for every claim. Then check what matters: open the source for every figure, price, date and quote, prefer original sources over articles about them, and mark anything you couldn't confirm. Deliver a one-page brief: the answer first, three to five key findings with links, options with pros and cons, a recommendation, and what you couldn't confirm. Recurring research, such as a monthly competitor watch, becomes a retainer service.

Research requests come in every shape: find three venues for the team offsite, compare these two software tools, what are competitors charging, who should we invite to the panel. A VA used to spend hours on these. With AI, the searching and summarizing take minutes, which leaves you time for the parts that matter: asking the right question and checking the answer.

How do you turn a vague request into a research question?

Before you search, write down five things, and confirm them with the client if anything's unclear:

  1. The question, in one sentence.
  2. The decision it helps them make.
  3. The scope: location, budget, dates, must-haves.
  4. The deadline, and how much time it's worth.
  5. The format they want: a short list, a comparison table, a recommendation.

"Look into CRM tools" becomes "Which two CRM tools under $50 a user a month would suit a five-person sales team already using Google Workspace, so you can pick one to trial next month?" That one sentence saves hours.

How do you search with AI?

Use an AI tool with web search switched on, so it reads current pages rather than relying on what it learned in training. Give it the full question, the scope and the format. Ask it to cite a source for every claim and to say when it couldn't find something. For bigger questions, use the tool's deeper research mode if it has one, and expect it to take a few minutes.

Give it your own material too: the client's notes, past emails about the topic, a shortlist they've already made. Research that starts from what the client knows is more useful than research that starts from zero.

How do you check what AI tells you?

This is the step that makes your research worth paying for. AI can misread a page, mix up two sources, or state something that isn't in the source at all.

  • Open the source for every fact that matters: prices, dates, figures, quotes, legal or medical claims.
  • Prefer the original: the company's own pricing page, the official statistics, the actual report, not an article summarizing it.
  • Check the date. A price from two years ago isn't today's price.
  • Mark what you couldn't confirm instead of leaving it out or presenting it as fact.

For a typical brief, checking takes longer than searching. That's normal, and it's the part a client can't easily do themselves.

What goes in a one-page research brief?

  • The answer first, in one or two sentences.
  • Three to five key findings, each with a link to its source.
  • The options, in a short table with pros, cons and cost.
  • Your recommendation, and why.
  • What you couldn't confirm, and how they could find out.

Keep it to one page. If there's more, put it in an appendix the client can skip. A brief that's easy to act on gets you more research work. A long report that sits unread doesn't.

How do you turn research into a recurring service?

Some research is worth doing every month. A competitor watch tracks a client's main competitors' prices, offers and announcements. A lead list finds new prospects that match the client's ideal customer. A news digest summarizes what changed in their industry. Each one becomes a monthly retainer, priced as shown in the guide to charging for AI work. Research is also one of the AI services VAs can sell on its own.

Which prompts should you use?

The first sharpens the request:

1

Turn a request into a research question

Prompt

My client asked me: "[paste request]". Context: [what I know about their business and why they might be asking]. Step 1: Rewrite the request as one clear research question. Step 2: State the decision it helps them make. Step 3: List the scope details I need (location, budget, dates, must-haves) and mark which ones I'm missing. Step 4: Write a two-line message asking the client to confirm the question and fill in the gaps.

The second runs the research with sources:

2

Research with sources

Prompt

Search the web to answer this question: [question]. Scope: [location, budget, dates, must-haves]. Step 1: Find the most relevant, current sources, preferring official and original ones. Step 2: For every claim, give the source link and the date of the information. Step 3: Where sources disagree, show both. Step 4: List anything you couldn't find or confirm. Step 5: Summarize the options in a table with pros, cons and cost. Don't state anything you didn't find in a source.

The third writes the brief once you've checked the facts:

3

Write the one-page research brief

Prompt

Write a one-page research brief for my client from my checked notes: [paste findings with sources, and mark which I've confirmed]. Sections: the answer in two sentences, three to five key findings with links, options table, my recommendation and why, what I couldn't confirm. Use only confirmed findings as facts, and put anything unconfirmed in the last section. Plain English, under 400 words.

Make the checking visible. A line at the bottom of each brief saying "every figure checked against its source on [date]" tells the client exactly what they're paying you for.

Frequently asked questions

How do virtual assistants use AI for research?

They turn the client's request into one clear question, use an AI tool with web search to find and summarize sources, check every figure, date and quote against the original source, and deliver a one-page brief with the answer first, key findings with links, options and a recommendation.

Can you trust AI research?

Not without checking. AI can misread or mix up sources and sometimes states things the source doesn't say. Open the source for every fact that matters, prefer original sources, check the date, and mark anything you couldn't confirm.

What should a research brief for a client include?

The answer in one or two sentences, three to five key findings with source links, a short options table with pros, cons and cost, a recommendation, and a list of what couldn't be confirmed. Keep it to one page.

What recurring research services can a VA offer?

A monthly competitor watch covering prices, offers and announcements, a lead list of new prospects matching the client's ideal customer, and an industry news digest. Each can be sold as a monthly retainer.

How long should AI-assisted research take?

The searching and summarizing often take minutes. Checking the facts usually takes longer than the search, which is normal and is the part clients value most.
