What should a freelancer's website say to turn visitors into clients?

A freelance website doesn't need to be beautiful. It needs to answer four questions fast: who is this for, what do I get, can I trust them, and what do I do next. Here's what to put on each page, and what to delete.

By Denise Cullom · October 29, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

Answer four questions, fast: who this is for, what result they get, why they can trust you, and what to do next. Put the first two in the first screen, in plain words a client would use. Keep it to five pages at most (home, services or offer, about, proof, contact) with one job each. Show proof in the form of client results, not adjectives, and give a price or a starting range so people can self-select. End every page with one next step, the same one, usually booking a short call. Write it in your own voice, then cut a third of the words.

Freelancers lose more work to a confusing website than to a plain one. A prospect lands on your page from a referral, a comment or a search, gives it about ten seconds, and decides whether you're for them. Nothing in those ten seconds has to be clever. It has to be clear.

When I was hiring, I'd open a site, scan the top of the homepage, look for someone like us in the proof, hunt for a price, and leave if I couldn't find any of it. Most buyers do the same thing.

What has to be in the first screen?

Before any scrolling, a visitor should see:

  • Who you help and what result you get them, in one line. This is your positioning sentence, from choosing a niche and writing an offer.
  • One or two lines of what that means in practice.
  • One button: book a call, get the free thing, or see the offer.
  • A face, ideally yours. People hire people.

What doesn't belong there: a slogan nobody can decode, a slideshow, a “welcome to my website,” or a paragraph about your journey. That comes later, if at all.

What pages does a freelancer actually need?

Five, and some people need three:

  1. Home. Who you help, the result, how it works in three steps, proof, and the next step.
  2. Services or offer. One page per main offer, or one page with two or three clearly named offers.
  3. About. Written for the client, not as a resume.
  4. Proof. Client results, case studies or testimonials, if you have enough for a page. Otherwise scatter them.
  5. Contact. One form or one booking link, plus an email address.

Blog posts and free downloads earn their place when they bring people in, like a useful guide or a checklist. A portfolio of pretty pictures with no context rarely does.

How do you write a services page that sells?

Use the same order a good conversation follows:

  1. The problem, in their words. If they don't see themselves in the first paragraph, nothing else gets read.
  2. The result, described so they can picture a specific week of their life being different.
  3. What's included, as outcomes rather than a list of tasks.
  4. How it works, in three or four steps, so the process feels safe.
  5. Who it's for and who it isn't. Saying who it isn't for builds more trust than any badge.
  6. Price or range, and what happens after they say yes.
  7. Proof from someone like them.
  8. The next step, with a button.

Then answer the three or four questions you get asked on every call. An FAQ at the bottom of a services page does a surprising amount of selling, and it's also what AI assistants quote when someone asks them a question like yours.

Should you put prices on your website?

Usually yes, at least a starting point. “Projects start at $1,200” or “retainers from $800 a month” saves you calls with people who were never going to buy, and makes you look confident to the ones who will. Showing the price of a small, fixed first step is even better.

The exception is genuinely bespoke consulting where the scope changes every time. Even then, give a range or say what a typical engagement looks like. “Contact for pricing” on its own reads as expensive and awkward, and busy buyers just close the tab.

What proof actually convinces people?

In order of power: a specific result with a number, a testimonial that names the before and after, a short case study about someone like the reader, logos of places you've worked, and finally, qualifications. Most freelance sites do that list backwards.

Three strong pieces of proof beat twenty weak ones. If you don't have any yet, say what you've done elsewhere, and get your first two testimonials using these three questions.

What should your about page say?

It's the second-most-visited page on most freelance sites, because people want to know who they'd be working with. Write it in this order: the kind of person you help and why you care about their problem, what you've done that makes you good at it, one or two human details, and the next step. Keep the childhood stories out unless they explain why you do this work.

What should every page end with?

The same single next step. Pick one: book a 15-minute call, buy the thing, or get the free download. Two options halve your results, and three means nobody does anything. If booking a call is the step, link straight to your calendar with a sentence about what happens on it, so nobody has to wonder whether it's a sales pitch.

How can AI help you write your site?

The hardest part of writing your own site is that you're too close to it. AI is useful for pulling the plain words out of what you already say to clients, and brutal for cutting. Start by giving it the raw material:

1

Draft your homepage from your own words

Prompt

My voice rules: [paste]. I help [one sentence]. Here's how I explain my work when someone asks at a party: [paste, written how you'd say it]. My best client results are [paste two or three]. My offer is [name, what's included, price]. Write my homepage with: a headline under 12 words saying who I help and the result, two supporting lines, how it works in three steps, where my proof goes and which result to use in each spot, one closing section with a single call to action to [book a call / get the guide], and the text for that button. Plain words only, no "elevate," "empower," "bespoke" or "passionate." Give me two versions of the headline: one plain, one with a bit more personality.

Then build the services page around the questions you're actually asked:

2

Write the services page and its FAQ

Prompt

My voice rules: [paste]. Write a services page for my offer: [name, what's included, timeline, price]. Audience: [who it's for]. Use this order: the problem in their words, the result they get, what's included as outcomes, how it works in four steps, who it's for and who it isn't, the price and what happens after they say yes, where to place proof, and the next step. Then write an FAQ of six questions my buyers really ask, using these as a starting point: [paste the questions you get on calls]. Answer each in two or three sentences. Under 700 words in total before the FAQ.

And once you have a draft, make something cut it:

3

Cut and sharpen the draft

Prompt

Here is a page from my website: [paste]. Step 1: Tell me, in ten seconds of reading, who this is for, what they get and what they're meant to do next. If any of the three isn't obvious, say so. Step 2: Cut it by a third without losing meaning, keeping my voice and removing filler, hedging and anything about me that doesn't help the reader decide. Step 3: List any claim I'd need proof for. Step 4: Suggest three places a client's own words would work better than mine, and what kind of quote would fit each one.

Read the result out loud before it goes live. If a sentence would embarrass you to say to a client's face, it won't help on the page either.

Frequently asked questions

What pages does a freelancer's website need?

Five at most: home, a services or offer page, about, proof, and contact. Each should have one job, and every page should end with the same single next step.

What should be at the top of a freelancer's homepage?

Who you help and the result you get them, in one line, plus a sentence or two of detail, one button and a photo of you. Leave slogans, slideshows and your life story out of the first screen.

Should freelancers show prices on their website?

Usually yes, at least a starting point such as “projects start at $1,200.” It filters out people who were never going to buy and reads as confident. For genuinely bespoke work, give a range or describe a typical engagement rather than only “contact for pricing.”

What should a services page include?

The problem in the client's words, the result, what's included as outcomes, how it works in three or four steps, who it's for and who it isn't, the price and what happens next, proof from someone like them, and one call to action, followed by an FAQ of the questions you're really asked.

What kind of proof works best on a freelance website?

A specific result with a number, then a testimonial naming the before and after, then a short case study about someone like the reader. Logos and qualifications matter least. Three strong pieces beat twenty weak ones.

What should a freelancer's about page say?

Who you help and why you care about their problem, what you've done that makes you good at it, one or two human details, and the next step. Write it for the client rather than as a resume.

Can AI write my website copy?

AI can draft and tighten your pages from your own words and the questions clients ask you, but it shouldn't invent claims or voice. Give it your writing rules and real client results, then cut anything you wouldn't say out loud.

Your site is one step in the chain. Work out who it's for, then bring people to it from LinkedIn and run the call well.
