How do virtual assistants use AI to plan travel and events for clients?

Booking a trip or planning a small event is mostly research, comparing and keeping track of details. AI is good at the first two and helps with the third. But a wrong flight time or a venue that's closed that day is the kind of mistake a client remembers. Here's how to use AI for the legwork and keep every detail right.

By Denise Cullom · November 17, 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer

Keep a traveler profile for each client: seat and hotel preferences, loyalty programs, budget limits, dietary needs, and what they hate. For each trip or event, write a one-page brief: dates, purpose, budget, must-haves. Use AI with web search to shortlist options with links, and to turn confirmed bookings into a one-page itinerary. Then confirm every time, address, booking reference and cancellation rule against the actual booking, not the AI's summary. You make the bookings yourself, with the client's approval. AI doesn't book or pay.

Travel and events are some of the most common jobs for executive and admin VAs, and some of the most time-consuming. A three-day work trip can take hours of tabs, comparisons and back-and-forth. AI can do most of the comparing in minutes. What it can't do is know that your client won't take a 6 a.m. flight, or confirm that the restaurant really has a private room on that date. That's still your job.

What goes in a traveler profile?

Write it once and update it after every trip. Store it in the client's shared folder, not inside an AI tool's memory.

  • Flights: preferred airlines and airports, seat, earliest and latest times they'll accept, layover limits.
  • Hotels: preferred chains, must-haves (gym, quiet room, near the venue), budget per night.
  • Ground travel: car hire, rideshare or train, and any rules on expenses.
  • Food: dietary needs, allergies, favorite places in cities they visit often.
  • Loyalty programs: the names, so bookings earn points. Keep membership numbers in the client's password manager or booking account, not in a prompt.
  • Dislikes: the things that make a trip go badly for them.

Passport numbers, dates of birth and payment details never go into an AI tool. The rules in keeping client data safe cover this.

What goes in a trip or event brief?

  1. The purpose: client meeting, conference, team offsite, dinner.
  2. Dates and fixed times: the meeting at 10 a.m. on Tuesday that everything else works around.
  3. Who's going and where they're coming from.
  4. The budget, in total or per person.
  5. Must-haves and nice-to-haves.
  6. Who approves the bookings, and by when.

How do you use AI to shortlist options?

Give an AI tool with web search the brief and the traveler profile, and ask for three options for each part: flights, hotels, venues, restaurants. Ask for a link and the date checked for each, and to say when it couldn't confirm a price or availability. Prices and availability change by the hour, so treat AI's figures as a starting point and confirm them on the booking site.

Send the client a short comparison: three options, a one-line reason for each, and your recommendation. Most clients just want to reply "go with option 2." The method in AI for research applies here too: original sources first, and mark what you couldn't confirm.

How do you build the itinerary?

Once everything is booked, paste the confirmation emails into the AI tool, with personal numbers removed, and ask for a one-page itinerary in time order. Each line: date, time, what, where, booking reference, and contact. Add travel time between stops, the time zone for each city, and a short "if something goes wrong" section: the airline's change line, the hotel's number, and your own hours.

Put it on the client's calendar too, with addresses in each event. If you manage their calendar (see AI for inbox and calendar management), block the travel time so nobody books a call over a flight.

How do you check that every detail is right?

This is the step that matters most. Check the itinerary against the actual booking confirmations, line by line, not against what the AI said.

  • Flight numbers, dates and times, in the local time zone.
  • Hotel check-in and check-out dates, and the address.
  • Booking references and names, spelled as on the ID.
  • Cancellation and change rules, and the deadline for each.
  • Opening hours for venues and restaurants on the actual day.

A useful habit: one day before departure, recheck flight times and send the client the itinerary again with any changes highlighted.

How do you plan a small event with AI?

For a team offsite, client dinner or workshop, the same brief works, with a few additions: number of guests, the format, and what the client wants people to leave with. AI helps with venue shortlists, a timeline counting back from the date, invitation and reminder emails, and a run sheet: the day minute by minute, who's responsible for what, and the supplier contacts. After the event, ask AI to draft the thank-you email and a short list of what to keep or change next time.

Keep the run sheet as a template. The next event takes half the time.

Which prompts should you use?

The first shortlists options:

1

Shortlist travel options

Prompt

Search the web for options for this trip. Brief: [purpose, dates, fixed times, cities, budget, must-haves]. Traveler preferences: [paste profile, with no personal numbers]. Give me three options each for flights and hotels. For each: link, price found, date checked, and one line on why it fits the brief. Flag anything that breaks a preference. Say clearly when you couldn't confirm a price or availability.

The second turns confirmations into an itinerary:

2

Build the one-page itinerary

Prompt

Turn these booking confirmations into a one-page itinerary in time order. Confirmations: [paste, with passport, payment and loyalty numbers removed]. For each line: date, local time and time zone, what, address, booking reference, contact. Add travel time between stops. End with an "if something goes wrong" section listing each company's contact. Use only details from the confirmations, and mark anything missing.

The third builds an event run sheet:

3

Write the event run sheet

Prompt

Write a run sheet for this event. Details: [date, venue, guest count, format, schedule, suppliers and contacts, who's helping]. Part 1: a timeline counting back from the event date with each task and owner. Part 2: the day itself, in 15-minute steps, with who is responsible for each step. Part 3: supplier contacts and what each is delivering. Part 4: a short list of what could go wrong and the backup plan for each.

Frequently asked questions

Can AI book travel for a client?

AI can shortlist and compare options and build the itinerary, but the VA should make the bookings, with the client's approval, on the actual booking sites. Don't give AI tools payment details, passport numbers or dates of birth.

What is a traveler profile?

A short document listing a client's travel preferences: airlines, seats, acceptable flight times, hotel must-haves, ground travel, food needs, loyalty program names and dislikes. It is written once, kept in the client's shared folder and updated after each trip.

How do you check an AI-built itinerary?

Compare every line with the booking confirmations: flight numbers and times in local time zones, hotel dates and address, names and booking references, cancellation deadlines, and opening hours on the day. Recheck flights the day before departure.

Are AI travel prices accurate?

Treat them as a starting point. Prices and availability change constantly, so confirm every price on the booking site before you recommend or book it.

How can AI help plan a small event?

It can shortlist venues, build a timeline counting back from the date, draft invitations and reminders, write a run sheet for the day, and draft the thank-you email afterward. The VA confirms bookings and suppliers directly.
