How do you keep your pipeline full when you're busy with client work?

The reason freelance income swings is simple: marketing stops the moment client work gets busy. Here's a 30-minute weekly routine that keeps the pipeline moving even in your worst week, and the four numbers that tell you what to fix.

By Denise Cullom · October 31, 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

Put one 30-minute slot in the calendar every week and treat it like a client meeting. In it, do four things: review your tracker and move each person forward a step, send the follow-ups that are due, add five new names, and start one new conversation. Watch four numbers: new conversations started, calls booked, proposals sent and work won. When income swings, it's almost always because marketing stopped during a busy month, and the gap shows up six to eight weeks later. The routine matters more than the volume: 30 honest minutes every week beats a frantic push every quarter.

Almost every freelancer knows this cycle. Work is quiet, so you market hard. It works, and you get busy. Being busy, you stop marketing. Two months later the project ends and the pipeline is empty, so you market hard again, usually while worrying about money, which is the worst possible state to sell from.

The cycle isn't caused by laziness. It's caused by marketing being the only thing in your week with no deadline attached. The fix is to give it one.

Why does freelance work come in waves?

Because there's a lag between the work you do to find clients and the money it produces. A conversation you start today might become a call in two weeks, a proposal in three, and paid work in six. So the quiet month you're having now was created by the busy month you had two months ago, when you had no time to talk to anyone new.

You can't remove the lag. You can keep a trickle going through the busy weeks so the drought never arrives.

What is the 30-minute weekly pipeline routine?

Same day, same time, every week. Here's the whole thing:

  1. Review the tracker (10 minutes). Go line by line. For every person, either take the next action now, schedule it, or close them out and move on. Nothing stays undecided.
  2. Send the follow-ups (10 minutes). Everyone who's due a touch this week, using the give-first approach from following up with prospects who stopped replying.
  3. Add five names (5 minutes). Five people who fit your niche, from a referral, a group, a trigger event or your network. The list never empties.
  4. Start one conversation (5 minutes). One genuine message to one new person. One a week is 50 a year.

That's it. In a brutal week, do step 1 and step 2 only. The point is that the slot never disappears.

What should you track?

A spreadsheet with one row per person is plenty: name, where they came from, what stage they're at, the last thing that happened, the next action and its date. Stages can be as simple as: to contact, contacted, in conversation, call booked, proposal sent, won, not now.

The “not now” list matters more than people think. Those are warm people with the wrong timing, and a check-in a few months later converts far better than a cold message ever will.

What are the four numbers to watch?

Count these monthly. They take two minutes and tell you exactly where the problem is:

  • New conversations started. The top of everything. If this is zero, nothing downstream can save you.
  • Calls booked. From those conversations.
  • Proposals sent. From those calls.
  • Work won. From those proposals.

Where the drop-off is tells you what to fix:

  • Few conversations: it's a volume and habit problem. Do the weekly slot, and use your warm network first.
  • Conversations but no calls: your messages are too vague, or you're asking for too much too early. Make the first line specific and the ask small, as in writing a cold message that gets a reply.
  • Calls but no proposals: you're pitching instead of diagnosing. Spend the call on their problem and what it costs them, per running a discovery call.
  • Proposals but no wins: price is landing as a surprise, or the next step is vague. Talk money on the call and always book the decision call, as in pricing and proposals.

How do you keep marketing during a busy month?

  • Shrink, don't skip. Fifteen minutes is a bad week. Zero is how droughts start.
  • Book it like a client. It's a recurring calendar event with your own name on it, not a “when I get a chance.”
  • Lean on the warm list. When you have no energy for new people, message past clients and contacts. It's less work and converts better.
  • Mine the work you're doing. A busy month is full of material: results, questions, lessons. One of those is this week's post.
  • Raise your prices. If you're too busy to market for months at a time, the real problem is that you're working too cheaply.

How much pipeline is enough?

A rough rule: you want roughly three times the value of the work you need in active conversations, because most won't close. If you need $5,000 of new work next quarter, you want about $15,000 of live opportunities moving. And aim for no single client to be more than about a third of your income, so losing one is a bad month instead of an emergency.

How can AI run the routine with you?

The weekly slot fails when it starts with a blank screen and a decision about who to chase. AI can remove both. Use this at the start of the session, pasting whatever your tracker says:

1

Prepare your weekly pipeline session

Prompt

Here is my pipeline tracker: [paste the rows, or export the sheet]. Today is [date]. I help [one sentence]. Step 1: List everyone whose next action is due or overdue, in priority order, with one line on why each matters. Step 2: For each one, say exactly what the next action should be and draft it in my voice ([paste voice rules]) in under 70 words. Step 3: Flag anyone who's gone quiet for more than four weeks and should move to a "not now" list with a date to check back. Step 4: Tell me the one thing in this pipeline I'm most likely to be avoiding. Keep the whole thing short enough to work through in 30 minutes.

Once a month, look at the numbers instead of the names:

2

Review the month's numbers

Prompt

Here are my numbers for the last three months: new conversations started, calls booked, proposals sent and work won: [paste]. My offer is [offer] at [price], and my target is [income goal] a month. Step 1: Work out the conversion rate at each step and tell me where the biggest drop-off is. Step 2: Tell me what that drop-off usually means and the two things to change first. Step 3: Work out how many new conversations a month I need to hit my target at my current conversion rates. Step 4: Give me one realistic weekly target to fix the weakest step. Be blunt rather than encouraging.

And keep the list topped up without it becoming a research project:

3

Find this week's five names

Prompt

I help [one sentence]. My best clients so far have been [describe two or three]. Step 1: List ten places these people gather or can be found: groups, communities, events, directories, job boards, podcasts, or types of search I could run. Be specific, not generic. Step 2: For each one, tell me what a good reason to reach out would look like there, and what would be inappropriate. Step 3: Give me a five-minute weekly habit for adding five new names from these sources to my tracker, with what to record for each. Step 4: List three trigger events that mean someone in my niche needs help right now and how I'd spot them.

None of this works if the slot isn't in your calendar. Put it there before you close this page: same day, same time, 30 minutes, repeating. That one calendar entry is the difference between a business and a series of lucky months.

How much time a week should I spend on marketing?

What should I track in a pipeline tracker?

One row per person with their name, where they came from, the stage they're at, the last thing that happened, the next action and its date. Keep a “not now” list too, because warm people with bad timing convert much better later than cold ones.

What numbers should a freelancer watch?

Four: new conversations started, calls booked, proposals sent and work won. Where the biggest drop-off happens tells you whether to fix your volume, your messages, your calls or your pricing and next steps.

How full should my pipeline be?

A rough rule is about three times the value of the work you need, since most opportunities don't close. Also try to keep any single client below about a third of your income so losing one isn't an emergency.

How do I keep marketing when I'm busy with client work?

Book the slot as a recurring calendar event, shrink it rather than skipping it, lean on your warm network when energy is low, and use the work you're doing as material. If you're too busy to market for months at a time, your prices are probably too low.
