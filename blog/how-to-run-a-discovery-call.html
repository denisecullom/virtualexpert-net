How do you run a discovery call that turns into a paying client?

A good discovery call feels less like a pitch and more like a doctor's visit. Here's the structure I use, the questions that matter most, how to bring up price without flinching, and the AI prompts that help you prepare, practice and follow up.

By Denise Cullom · October 20, 2026 · 10 min read

The short answer

Spend most of the call on their problem, not on what you do. Ask what their biggest problems are and what each one costs them every month, then ask what a great result would look like a year from now. Play it back in their own words until they say “yes, that's it.” Only then recommend one small first step, give its price next to the cost of the problem, and stop talking. Ask one plain question to decide, and never hang up without a next step on the calendar: the kickoff if it's a yes, a short decision call if they need time. AI can do your prep in five minutes, play the prospect so you can rehearse, and write the recap after.

Most freelancers treat a discovery call as a chance to explain everything they do. They walk through their services, their process and their experience, and then wait for the buyer to say yes. The buyer says “this sounds great, let me think about it,” and that's the last you hear from them.

In my years running sales and operations teams, the best closers I worked with talked the least on their calls. They asked better questions, wrote down the answers, and by the end the buyer had talked themselves into the next step. That's a skill you can learn, and it starts with having a structure, so you're not making it up as you go.

What is a discovery call, really?

A discovery call is a conversation where you find out whether someone has a problem you can solve, what that problem is costing them, and whether they're ready to fix it. If all three are true, the call ends with a clear recommendation and a next step. If they aren't, it ends kindly and early, and you've both saved a lot of time.

The goal isn't to impress them. It's to help them see their own situation more clearly than they did before the call. When that happens, hiring you stops feeling like a sales decision and starts feeling like the obvious thing to do.

Should you do one call or two?

For most service businesses, two calls work better than one:

  • A 15-minute fit call. A quick check of four things: you'd enjoy working together, their expectations are realistic, they have a specific problem you solve, and they're ready to act (they can decide, it matters now and they expect to invest). If any of those is missing, you say so kindly and point them to something useful.
  • A 45-minute discovery call. Only for people who passed the fit call. This is where you go deep, play it back and recommend a path, including the price.

The short first call protects your calendar. You stop spending 45 minutes with people who were “just curious,” and the people who reach the longer call arrive already interested. If your offer is small and simple, one 30-minute call that does both is fine.

How should you structure a 45-minute discovery call?

Here's the outline I use. Print it, keep it beside you, and glance at the clock now and then.

  1. Reconnect (3 minutes). Bring up something they told you last time. It shows you listened, and it warms the room.
  2. Set the agenda (2 minutes). Tell them how the call will go: first their situation, then where they want to be, then, only if it makes sense, how you'd help and what it costs. Promise they'll leave with a clearer picture either way.
  3. Where they are (12 minutes). Their three biggest problems, what's causing each one, what they've tried, and what each one costs them.
  4. Where they want to be (8 minutes). What a great year looks like, and the one number that would prove it worked.
  5. Play it back (5 minutes). Summarize their problems, cost and goal in their own words, and wait for a clear yes.
  6. Recommend a path (10 minutes). One small first step, the result it gets, and the price.
  7. Decide (5 minutes). Ask for the decision, or book a short decision call.

Notice that you don't talk about yourself until minute 30. That's on purpose. Aim for them talking 70% of the time and you 30%, and write down their exact words about the problem. You'll need them later.

What questions should you ask?

You don't need a long list. You need a few good questions and the patience to follow each answer with “tell me more.” For each problem they name, work through these:

  • “What's causing that?”
  • “How long has it been going on?”
  • “What have you already tried?”
  • “Roughly what does it cost you each month, in money, time or lost business?”
  • “If nothing changes, what does this look like a year from now?”

The cost question is the most important one on the call. Most people have never put a number on their problem, and saying it out loud changes how they see it. Write the number down. It becomes the yardstick for your price.

Then flip to the future: “Imagine it's a year from now and this went really well. What changed? What number would tell you it worked?” Before you wrap up this part, ask who else is involved in the decision, so you don't find out about a business partner after you've sent the proposal.

How do you play it back?

This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that does the selling. Before you recommend anything, summarize what you heard in their language. It sounds like this:

“Let me make sure I've got this right. Right now you're answering client emails late every night, your invoices go out two weeks late, and you haven't had time to market the business since spring. You think it's costing you around $3,000 a month in late payments and lost work, and you're exhausted. A year from now you want evenings back and two new retainer clients a quarter. Did I get that right?”

Then wait. If they say yes, they've just agreed, in their own words, that the problem is real and worth fixing. If they correct you, that's even better, because now you truly understand it.

How do you bring up price on the call?

Talk about price on the call, not only in the proposal. A price that shows up for the first time in a PDF is a surprise, and surprises are what make buyers go quiet. Three things make it feel natural:

  1. Sell the first step, not the whole project. If you price everything you could ever do for them, they freeze. Recommend one small, clear step with a result they'll see quickly, and decide on the rest together once it's working.
  2. Put the price next to their number. “The first step is $1,200. You said this is costing you about $3,000 a month, so it costs less than two weeks of the problem.”
  3. Then stop talking. The silence after a price feels long. Let it be long. The next person to speak should be them.

How do you end the call?

Ask one plain question: “Based on everything we've talked through, does this feel like the right next step for you?” Then be quiet and let them answer.

  • If it's a yes: book the kickoff while you're still on the call, and tell them the agreement and invoice will arrive within the hour.
  • If they need time: that's fine, but don't agree to “follow up sometime.” Book a 20-minute decision call two or three days out, and ask them to think about what success looks like, what waiting costs them, and any questions still on their mind.
  • If it isn't a fit: say so honestly, and offer a resource or a referral. People remember the person who didn't try to sell them something they didn't need.

The rule underneath all three: never end a call without a next step on the calendar. A booked call is a conversation that continues. “I'll be in touch” is a conversation that's already over.

What do you say when they hesitate?

Hesitation is information, not rejection. Something is in the way, and your job is to find out what. Three moves work for almost every objection:

  1. Notice it out loud. “It seems like something's on your mind. What is it?”
  2. Check it's the only thing. “That's fair. Is that the only thing holding you back, or is there something else?”
  3. Solve that one thing. “If we could sort that out, would you want to go ahead?”

For “it's more than I expected,” offer a smaller scope instead of a lower price for the same work. For “I need to talk to my partner,” offer to join a short call with both of them or send a one-page summary they can share. For “I need to think about it,” ask what specifically they want to think through, and answer it now if you can.

How do you use AI before and after the call?

AI won't run the call for you, and it shouldn't. But it's excellent at the three jobs around the call that most people rush or skip: preparing, practicing and following up. Start with prep, using what you know from their website, their posts and the fit call:

1

Prepare for the call

Prompt

You are helping me prepare for a 45-minute discovery call. I am a [your role] who helps [type of client] with [result you deliver]. My first-step offer is [offer] at [price]. I'm meeting [name], [role] at [company]. Here's what I know about them from their website, their posts and our 15-minute fit call: [paste notes]. Step 1: List the three problems they most likely have that I can solve, and what each might be costing them each month. Step 2: Write eight open-ended questions in the order I should ask them, starting with their situation, then cost, then the result they want, then who else is involved in the decision. Step 3: List the two objections I'm most likely to hear and an honest, low-pressure response to each. Step 4: Suggest the smallest sensible first step I could recommend if the call goes well. Keep it to one page I can print.

Then practice out loud. Reading questions is not the same as asking them to a real person who pushes back, and the first few times you say a price out loud should not be on a real call:

2

Rehearse with a skeptical prospect

Prompt

Play the role of [name], a [role] at a [type of business] who is interested in hiring a [your role] but is busy, a little skeptical and careful with money. Your real problems are [one or two problems from my prep]. Don't make it easy: give short answers at first, only open up when I ask good follow-up questions, and when I mention my price of [price], raise one realistic objection. I'll run my discovery call and you respond as the prospect, one message at a time. After 12 exchanges, step out of the role and give me feedback: what percentage of the time I was talking, which questions worked best, where I missed a chance to ask about cost, and how I handled the price and the objection.

After the call, give yourself 30 minutes to send the next email and update your tracker while it's fresh. If they need time, the recap is what keeps the conversation alive, because it hands their own words back to them:

3

Turn your notes into a recap email

Prompt

Here are my rough notes from a discovery call with [name] at [company]: [paste notes]. Step 1: Summarize them under these headings: situation, top three problems and what each costs, the result they want and the number they'll measure it by, decision makers, concerns, and the next step we agreed. Step 2: Flag anything I didn't find out that I should ask before sending a proposal. Step 3: Write a recap email under 150 words in a warm, direct voice that thanks them, plays back their problems and goal using their own words from my notes, mentions the cost of waiting, and confirms [next step, such as our decision call on Thursday at 2pm]. No exclamation marks and no phrases like “circling back.”

If you record your calls with a notetaker app, you can paste the transcript instead of your notes. Ask the prospect before you record, and leave out anything confidential they haven't agreed to share with an AI tool.

What mistakes lose clients on a discovery call?

  • Pitching in the first ten minutes. Until you understand their problem, anything you say about yourself is a guess.
  • Never asking what the problem costs. Without a number, your price has nothing to be compared to except other freelancers.
  • Proposing the whole project. A big number for a long list of work is easy to put off. A small first step is easy to say yes to.
  • Filling the silence after the price. Rushing in with a discount before they've even answered tells them the price was negotiable.
  • Leaving without a date. “Send me a proposal and I'll take a look” is where good deals go to wait forever. If that happens, use these follow-up timings.
  • Missing a decision maker. If a partner or boss has a say, invite them to the call, not to an email afterwards.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a discovery call be?

Most freelancers and consultants do well with two steps: a 15-minute fit call to check that the person has a real problem you solve and is ready to act, then a 45-minute discovery call to go deep and recommend a path. For small, simple offers a single 30-minute call can cover both.

What questions should you ask on a discovery call?

Ask about the problem before anything else: what the three biggest things holding them back are, what is causing each one, what they have tried, and roughly what each problem costs them each month. Then ask where they want to be a year from now and what number would prove it worked. Finish by asking who else is involved in the decision and how important solving this is right now.

Should you talk about price on a discovery call?

Yes, once you understand the problem and what it costs them. Give one price for one clear first step, compare it to their monthly cost of the problem, then stop talking. Avoiding price makes the proposal feel like a surprise, and surprises are what make buyers go quiet.

How do you close at the end of a discovery call without being pushy?

Ask one plain question, such as “Does this feel like the right next step for you?”, and wait for the answer. If it is yes, book the kickoff before you hang up. If they need time, book a short decision call two or three days out instead of agreeing to “follow up sometime.”

What should you do if a prospect says “I need to think about it”?

Treat it as information, not rejection. Ask what specifically they want to think through, and answer it on the spot if you can. If they still need time, put a 20-minute decision call on the calendar for later that week so the conversation has a clear end point.
